# Solutions of Maxwell's Equations

## Maxwell's Equations in Static Limit

Also called steady state equations, stationary equations

Conditions: $ \dfrac{\partial \mathbf E}{\partial t} = \mathbf 0$, $ \dfrac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t} = \mathbf 0$

Solutions:

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf E = \dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \quad \nabla \times \mathbf E = \mathbf 0 \quad \text{(Electrostatic equations)}
$$

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf B = 0, \quad \nabla \times \mathbf B = \mu_0 \mathbf J \quad \text{(Magnetostatic equations)}
$$

## Charge Conservation

The total charge in an isolated system is conserved.

$$
    \dfrac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf J = 0 \quad \text{(Continuity Equation of Charge Conservation)}
$$

$$
    \implies \dfrac{\mathrm d Q}{\mathrm d t} = - I
$$

where $I$ is the total electric current passing through a volume $V$ and $Q$ is the total electric charge enclosed in $V$.

In an isolated system,

$$
    I = 0 \implies Q = \text{constant}
$$

## Electromagnetic Wave Equation

Conditions: $\rho = 0$, $\mathbf J = \mathbf 0$

Solutions:

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf E = 0, \quad \nabla \times \mathbf E + \dfrac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t} = \mathbf 0
$$

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf B = 0, \quad \nabla \times \mathbf B - \dfrac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial \mathbf E}{\partial t} = \mathbf 0
$$

$$
    \implies \dfrac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf E}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf E = \mathbf 0, \quad \dfrac{1}{c^2} \dfrac{\partial^2 \mathbf B}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2 \mathbf B = \mathbf 0
$$

where

$$
    c = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\varepsilon_0 \mu_0}}
$$

## Energy in an Electromagnetic Field

The electromagnetic energy density is

$$
    u_{em} = u_e + u_m
$$

In vacuum,

$$
    u_{em} = \dfrac{1}{2} \left( \varepsilon_0 \Vert \mathbf E \Vert^2 + \dfrac{\Vert \mathbf B \Vert^2}{\mu_0} \right)
$$

In matter,

$$
    u_{em} = \dfrac{1}{2} (\mathbf E \cdot \mathbf D + \mathbf H \cdot \mathbf B)
$$

$$
    \implies u_{em} = \dfrac{1}{2} \left( \varepsilon \Vert \mathbf E \Vert^2 + \dfrac{\Vert \mathbf B \Vert^2}{\mu} \right) \quad \text{(For linear materials)}
$$

## Poynting Vector

The Poynting vector $\mathbf S$ is given by

$$
    \mathbf S = \dfrac{1}{\mu_0} \mathbf E \times \mathbf B
$$

## Poynting Flux

$$
    \Phi_S = \int_A \mathbf S \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf A
$$

where $A$ is a surface whose differential area element is $\mathrm d \mathbf A$

## Poynting's Theorem

The rate of energy transfer per unit volume from a region of space equals the rate of work done on the charge distribution in the region, plus the energy flux leaving that region.

$$
    - \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \int_V u_{em} \, \mathrm d V = \Phi_S + \int_V \mathbf J \cdot \mathbf E \, \mathrm d V
$$

$$
    \implies - \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \int_V u_{em} \, \mathrm d V = \oint_{\partial V} \mathbf S \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf A + \int_V \mathbf J \cdot \mathbf E \, \mathrm d V
$$

$$
    \implies \dfrac{\partial u_{em}}{\partial t} = \nabla \cdot \mathbf S + \mathbf J \cdot \mathbf E
$$

where $\int_V \mathbf J \cdot \mathbf E \, \mathrm d V$ is the work done on the charges by the fields.
