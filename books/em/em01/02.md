# Electric Potential

## Electric Potential

In electrostatics, electric potential is defined by

$$
\begin{aligned}
    & \boxed{\mathbf E = - \nabla V} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & V = - \int \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf \ell
\end{aligned}
$$

Electric potential is defined relative to a reference point $\mathbf r_0$.

$$
    \Delta V = V(\mathbf r) - V(\mathbf r_0) = - \int_{\mathbf r_0}^{\mathbf r} \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf \ell
$$

We usually take the reference point to be at infinity, where the potential is zero $(V(\infty) = 0)$.

$$
    \boxed{V(\mathbf r) = - \int_{\infty}^{\mathbf r} \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf \ell}
$$

**Voltage:** Voltage is the difference in electric potential between two points. In electrostatics, it is the work needed per unit of charge to move a positive test charge from the first point to the second point.

$$
    \Delta V \equiv V = V(\mathbf r_1) - V(\mathbf r_0) = - \int_{\mathbf r_0}^{\mathbf r_1} \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf \ell
$$

### Electric Potential of a Point Charge

The electric potential due to a point charge is called the Coulomb potential, and is given by

$$
    V = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r}
$$

### Electric Potential of Charge Distributions

#### Discrete Charge Distribution

$$
    \boxed{V (\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \sum_i \frac{q_i}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r_i |}}
$$

#### Continuous Charge Distribution

$$
\begin{aligned}
    & \boxed{\mathrm d V = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathrm d q}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r' |}} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & V (\mathbf r) = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\rho (\mathbf r')}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r' |} \mathrm d^3 r'
\end{aligned}
$$

## Irrotationality of Electrostatic Fields

$$
    \boxed{\nabla \times \mathbf E = \mathbf 0}
$$

## Poisson's Equation for Electrostatics

$$
    \boxed{\nabla^2 V = - \dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}}
$$

where $\rho$ is the charge density.

### Laplace's Equation

$$
    \nabla^2 V = 0
$$

## Electric Potentials of Common Charge Distributions

### Point Charge

$$
    \boxed{V = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r}}
$$

### Solid Sphere

$$
    \boxed{V =
        \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q (3R^2 - r^2)}{2 R^3} & , & r < R \\[2ex]
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r} & , & r \geq R
        \end{cases}}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed in the volume.

### Spherical Shell

$$
    \boxed{V =
        \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{R} & , & r < R \\[2ex]
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r} & , & r \geq R
        \end{cases}}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed on the surface.

### Infinite Line

$$
    \boxed{V = - \dfrac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0} \ln r}
$$

### Infinite Plane

$$
    \boxed{V = - \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} r}
$$

### Charged Ring

$$
    V = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed on the ring.

### Charged Disc

$$
    V = \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \left( \sqrt{x^2 + R^2} - x^2 \right) \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the uniform surface charge density.

## Electric Dipole

### Electric Dipole Moment

Electric dipole moment is a measure of the separation of the positive and negative charges within a system. For a pure dipole, the dipole moment is

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf p = q \mathbf r}
$$

where $\mathbf r$ is the separation vector pointing from $-q$ to $q$.

### Electric Field of a Dipole

Electric field due to a dipole at an observation point $r$ distance away from the center of the dipole is

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial plane)}
$$

where $p = 2aq$ is the electric dipole moment.

If the point is far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axis, far from the dipole)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial plane, far from the dipole)}
$$

In general, the vector equation of the electric field of an electric dipole for a point far away from the dipole is

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{3 (\mathbf p \cdot \hat{\mathbf r}) \hat{\mathbf r} - \mathbf p}{r^3}}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is the unit vector pointing from the dipole's center to the observation point.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a dipole parallel to the $x$-axis and centered at the origin. The dipole charges are, $q$ at $x = a$ and $-q$ at $x = -a$. So, the dipole moment is defined as

$$
    \mathbf p = q(2a) \hat{\mathbf x} \implies p = 2aq
$$

---

**Electric Field on the Axis:** Let $P$ be a point on the axis, at a position $x = r$ from the center, with $r > a$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \mathbf E_{+} + \mathbf E_{-} & = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{q}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{q}{(r + a)^2} \right] \hat{\mathbf x} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{4aqr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf x} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \boxed{\mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf x}} \qquad \text{(On the axial line)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p = 2aq$.

---

**Electric Field on the Equatorial Plane:** Let $P$ be a point on the equatorial line (i.e. the perpendicular bisector of the dipole), at a position $y = r$ from the center. The field magnitude from each charge is

$$
    E_q = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2}
$$

where $R = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$ is the distance from each charge.

By symmetry,

- The components of the electric field perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel.
- The components parallel to the dipole axis add.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
    & E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_q \cos \theta \\[0.4em]
    \implies & E_{\text{eq}} = 2 \left( \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2} \right) \frac{a}{R} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2aq}{R^3} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \boxed{E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line)}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p = 2aq$.

---

At a point far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

</div>

### Electric Potential of a Dipole

Electric potential due to a dipole at an observation point $r$ distance away from the center of the dipole, provided that the point is far away $(r \gg a)$ is
    
$$
    \boxed{V = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{p \cos \theta}{r^2}}
$$

where $p = 2aq$ is the electric dipole moment.

## Electrostatic Energy

### Electric Potential Energy

The potential energy of a charge moving between two points in an electric field is

$$
    \Delta U = q \Delta V = - q \int_{\mathbf r_0}^{\mathbf r} \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r
$$

If a charge is brought into the electric field from infinity, then

$$
    \boxed{U_e = q V(\mathbf r)}
$$

For a discrete charge distribution,

$$
    U_e = \frac{1}{2} \sum_i q_i V(\mathbf r)
$$

For a continuous charge distribution,

$$
    \boxed{U_e = \frac{1}{2} \int \rho V \, \mathrm d^3 r}
$$

where $\rho$ is the charge density and $V$ is the electric potential.

### Electric Field Energy

The potential energy stored in an electric field is

$$
    \boxed{U_e = \frac{\varepsilon_0}{2} \int E^2 \, \mathrm d^3 r}
$$

Therefore, the electric field energy density is

$$
    \boxed{u_e = \dfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon_0 \Vert \mathbf E \Vert^2}
$$
