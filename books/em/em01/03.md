# Electrostatics in Matter

## Electric Charge in Matter

In dielectric materials, the total charge $Q$ is composed of 2 types of charges $(Q = Q_f + Q_b)$:

**Bound Charge:** Bound charge $Q_b$ is the net accumulation of charge from the orientation of the dipoles created by stationary charges in the material. Bound charges cannot be removed from the material and they do not conduct electricity.

**Free Charge:** Free charge $Q_f$ is the net excess charge in the material that can move into electrostatic equilibrium. Free charges can move throughout the material and can conduct electricity.

### Charge Density in Matter

**Volume Charge Density:**

$$
    \rho = \rho_f + \rho_b
$$

where

- $\rho_f = \dfrac{\mathrm d Q_f}{\mathrm d V}$ is the volume free-charge density

- $\rho_b = \dfrac{\mathrm d Q_b}{\mathrm d V}$ is the volume bound-charge density

**Surface Charge Density:**

$$
    \sigma = \sigma_f + \sigma_b
$$

where

- $\sigma_f = \dfrac{\mathrm d Q_f}{\mathrm d S}$ is the surface free-charge density

- $\sigma_b = \dfrac{\mathrm d Q_b}{\mathrm d S}$ is the surface bound-charge density

## Electric Dipoles

### Electric Dipole Moment

Electric dipole moment is a measure of the separation of the positive and negative charges within a system. For a pure dipole, the dipole moment is

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf p = q \mathbf r}
$$

where $\mathbf r$ is the separation vector pointing from $-q$ to $q$.

### Electric Field of a Dipole

Electric field due to a dipole at an observation point $r$ distance away from the center of the dipole is

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial plane)}
$$

where $p = 2aq$ is the electric dipole moment.

If the point is far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axis, far from the dipole)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial plane, far from the dipole)}
$$

In general, the vector equation of the electric field of an electric dipole for a point far away from the dipole is

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{3 (\mathbf p \cdot \hat{\mathbf r}) \hat{\mathbf r} - \mathbf p}{r^3}}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is the unit vector pointing from the dipole's center to the observation point.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a dipole parallel to the $x$-axis and centered at the origin. The dipole charges are, $q$ at $x = a$ and $-q$ at $x = -a$. So, the dipole moment is defined as

$$
    \mathbf p = q(2a) \hat{\mathbf x} \implies p = 2aq
$$

---

**Electric Field on the Axis:** Let $P$ be a point on the axis, at a position $x = r$ from the center, with $r > a$. Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \mathbf E_{+} + \mathbf E_{-} & = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{q}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{q}{(r + a)^2} \right] \hat{\mathbf x} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{4aqr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf x} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \boxed{\mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf x}} \qquad \text{(On the axial line)}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p = 2aq$.

---

**Electric Field on the Equatorial Plane:** Let $P$ be a point on the equatorial line (i.e. the perpendicular bisector of the dipole), at a position $y = r$ from the center. The field magnitude from each charge is

$$
    E_q = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2}
$$

where $R = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}$ is the distance from each charge.

By symmetry,

- The components of the electric field perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel.
- The components parallel to the dipole axis add.

Therefore,

$$
\begin{aligned}
    & E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_q \cos \theta \\[0.4em]
    \implies & E_{\text{eq}} = 2 \left( \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2} \right) \frac{a}{R} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2aq}{R^3} \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \boxed{E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line)}}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $p = 2aq$.

---

At a point far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

### Electric Potential of a Dipole

Electric potential due to a dipole at an observation point $r$ distance away from the center of the dipole, provided that the point is far away $(r \gg a)$ is
    
$$
    \boxed{\phi = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{p \cos \theta}{r^2}}
$$

where $p = 2aq$ is the electric dipole moment.

## Polarization

### Polarization Density

Polarization density expresses the volumetric density of electric dipole moments in a dielectric material.

$$
    \mathbf P = \dfrac{\mathrm d \mathbf p}{\mathrm d V} = \rho_b \mathbf d
$$

where $\rho_b$ is the bound charge density and $\mathbf d$ is the separation vector (displacement of the charge due to an applied electric field).

#### Polarizability

Polarizability $\alpha$ is defined by

$$
    \mathbf p = \alpha \mathbf E
$$

where $\mathbf p$ is the induced electric dipole moment of an atom and $\mathbf E$ is the electric field that produces it.

### Gauss's Law for Polarization Density

$$
    \Phi_P = - Q_b
$$

$$
    \implies \oint_{\partial V} \mathbf P \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S = - \int_V \rho_b \, \mathrm d V
$$

$$
    \implies \nabla \cdot \mathbf P = - \rho_b
$$

where $Q_b$ is the total bound charge enclosed by the surface and $\rho_b$ is the bound charge density inside the surface.

## Electric Displacement Field

Electric displacement field is an auxiliary field that accounts for the electromagnetic effects of polarization and that of an electric field.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf D = \varepsilon \mathbf E + \mathbf P}
$$

In vacuum, there is no polarization, so

$$
    \mathbf D = \varepsilon_0 \mathbf E
$$

## Electric Displacement Flux

$$
    \boxed{\Phi_D = \int_S \mathbf D \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S}
$$

## Electric Field Energy in Matter

In matter,

$$
    u_e = \frac{1}{2} \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf D
$$

## Linear Dielectrics

### Electric Response Quantities

#### Permittivity

$$
    \varepsilon_r = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

#### Electric Susceptibility

A constant indicating the degree of polarization of a dielectric material in response to an electric field.

$$
    \chi_e = \varepsilon_r - 1 = \dfrac{\varepsilon - \varepsilon_0}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

In vacuum, $\chi_e = 0$

### Polarization Density in Linear Dielectrics

In linear materials, the polarization density is

$$
    \mathbf P = \chi_e \varepsilon_0 \mathbf E
$$

### Electric Displacement Field in Linear Dielectrics

In linear materials, the electric displacement field is

$$
    \mathbf D = ( 1 + \chi_e ) \mathbf E = \varepsilon \mathbf E
$$
