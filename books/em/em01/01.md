# Electric Field

## Electric Charge

$$
    Q = \int_V \rho_q \, \mathrm d V
$$

## Charge Density

Electric charge density (or volume charge density) is the charge per unit volume.

$$
    \rho_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d V}
$$

### Linear Charge Density

Linear charge density is the charge per unit length.

$$
    \lambda_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d l}
$$

### Surface Charge Density

Surface charge density is the charge per unit surface area.

$$
    \sigma_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d S}
$$

## Electric Field

Electric field is the physical field responsible for electric phenomena.

In electrostatics, it is defined as electric force per unit charge.

$$
    \mathbf E = \frac{\mathbf F_e}{q}
$$

## Electric Flux

Electric flux is the measure of electric field through a surface.

$$
    \Phi_E = \int_S \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S
$$

For a uniform electric field,

$$
    \Phi_E = \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf S
$$

## Coulomb's Law

Electric force for point charges is given by

$$
    \mathbf F_e = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Qq}{r^2} \mathbf{\hat r}
$$

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r^2} \mathbf{\hat r}
$$

**Assumptions of Coulomb's Law:**

- The charges must have a spherically symmetric distribution (e.g. point charge, uniformly charged sphere, etc.)
- The charges must not overlap (They must be distinct point charges).

## Electric Dipole

### Electric Dipole Moment

### Electric Field of a Dipole

Electric field due to a dipole at a point $r$ distance away from the center of the dipole is

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \qquad \text{(On the axial line)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line)}
$$

If the point is far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a dipole parallel to the $x$-axis and centered at the origin. The dipole charges are, $q$ at $x = a$ and $-q$ at $x = -a$. So, the dipole moment is defined as

$$
    \mathbf p = q(2a) \hat{\mathbf x} \implies p = 2aq
$$

**Electric Field on the Axial Line**

Let $P$ be a point on the axial line, at a position $x = r$ from the center, with $r > a$.

The field due to charge $q$ is

$$
    \mathbf E_{+} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{(r - a)^2} \hat{\mathbf x}
$$

The field due to charge $-q$ is

$$
    \mathbf E_{-} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{(r + a)^2} \hat{\mathbf x}
$$

Therefore,

$$
    \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \mathbf E_{+} + \mathbf E_{-} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \left[ \frac{q}{(r - a)^2} - \frac{q}{(r + a)^2} \right] \hat{\mathbf x}
$$

which is

$$
    \mathbf E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{4aqr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \hat{\mathbf x}
$$

Since $p = 2aq$, we therefore have

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2pr}{(r^2 - a^2)^2} \qquad \text{(On the axial line)}
$$

At a point far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{ax}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the axial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

**Electric Field on the Equatorial Line**

Let $P$ be a point on the equatorial line (i.e. the perpendicular bisector of the dipole), at a position $y = r$ from the center.

The distance from each charge is

$$
    R = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2}
$$

The field magnitude from each charge is

$$
    \mathbf E_q = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2}
$$

By symmetry, the components of the electric field perpendicular to the dipole axis cancel, and the components parralel to the dipole axis add. Therefore,

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = 2 E_q \cos \theta = 2 \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{q}{R^2} \frac{a}{R} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2aq}{R^3}
$$

Using $p = 2aq$, we get

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{(r^2 + a^2)^{3/2}} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line)}
$$

At a point far away from the dipole $(r \gg a)$,

$$
    E_{\text{eq}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{p}{r^3} \qquad \text{(On the equatorial line, far from the dipole)}
$$

### Torque on an Electric Dipole

Torque on an electric dipole due to an external electric field.

$$
    \boldsymbol \tau = \int_V \mathrm d \mathbf p \times \mathbf E = \int_V \mathrm d \mathbf r \times \mathbf F_e
$$

where $\mathbf p = q \mathbf r$ is the electric dipole moment.

## Electric Fields of Common Charge Distributions

### Infinite Wire:

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0 x} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

### Finite Wire:

### Infinitely Large Surface:
    
$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

### Infinitely Long Cylindrical Volume:

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0 x} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

### Spherical Volume:

Assuming total charge is uniformly distributed in the volume.

Outside the sphere:
    
$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{Q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 x^2} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

Inside the sphere:

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{Qr}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 R^3} \mathbf{\hat r}
$$

### Spherical Surface:

Assuming total charge is uniformly distributed on the surface.

Outside the sphere:
    
$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{Q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 x^2} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$
    
Inside the sphere:
    
$$
    \mathbf E = \mathbf 0
$$

### Charged Ring:

Total charge is uniformly distributed on the ring.

On the axis:

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{Qx}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 (R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

### Charged Disc:

On the axis:

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \left( 1 - \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + R^2}}\right) \mathbf{\hat x}
$$
