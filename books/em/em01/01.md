# Electric Field

## Electric Charge

Electric charge $Q$ is a fundamental physical property of matter that determines its interaction to the electromagnetic field.

### Charge Density

**Volume Charge Density:** Charge per unit volume.

$$
    \rho_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d V}
$$

**Linear Charge Density:** Charge per unit length.

$$
    \lambda_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d l}
$$

**Surface Charge Density:** Charge per unit surface area.

$$
    \sigma_q = \dfrac{\mathrm d q}{\mathrm d S}
$$

## Electric Field

Electric field $\mathbf E$ is the physical field responsible for electric phenomena. It is a vector field and is produced by electric charges.

### Electric Force

In electrostatics, electric force is the force exerted by an electric field on a charged particle.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf F_e = q \mathbf E}
$$

## Coulomb's Law

Coulomb's law assumes the following:

- The charges have a spherically symmetric distribution (e.g. point charge, uniformly charged sphere, etc.)
- The charges do not overlap (They must be distinct point charges).

Coulomb's law describes the electric force for point charges.

$$
    \mathbf F_e = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Qq}{r^2} \mathbf{\hat r}
$$

where
- $\mathbf r$ is the position of the observation point (where you want the field)
- $\mathbf r'$ is the position of the source charge element
- $\mathrm d q$ is the infinitesimal source charge
- $\rho(\mathbf r')$ is the volume charge density

## Electric Flux

Electric flux is the measure of electric field through a surface.

$$
    \Phi_E = \int_S \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S
$$

For a uniform electric field,

$$
    \Phi_E = \mathbf E \cdot \mathbf S
$$

## Gauss's Law

The electric flux out of an arbitrary closed surface (called a **Gaussian surface**) is proportional to the electric charge enclosed by the surface.

$$
    \boxed{\Phi_E = \dfrac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}}
$$

$$
    \implies \oint_{\partial V} \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S = \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_0} \int_V \rho \, \mathrm d V
$$

$$
    \implies \nabla \cdot \mathbf E = \dfrac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge enclosed by the closed surface and $\rho$ is the charge density inside the surface.

## Electric Field for Charge Distributions

For any charge distribution, the electric field is

$$
    \boxed{\mathrm d \mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\mathbf r - \mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3} \mathrm d q}
$$

$$
    \implies \mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\mathbf r - \mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3} \mathrm d q
    \implies \mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \int \frac{\mathbf r - \mathbf r'}{|\mathbf r - \mathbf r'|^3} \, \rho(\mathbf r') \, \mathrm d V
$$

## Electric Fields of Common Charge Distributions

### Point Charge

Already discussed in **Coulomb's Law**.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r^2} \mathbf{\hat r}}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a point charge $q$ located at the origin. Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ centered on the charge.

By symmetry:

- $\mathbf E$ is radial.
- $E$ is constant over the Gaussian sphere.

Apply Gauss's law:

$$
    \Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Since the enclosed charge is $Q$, the flux is

$$
    \Phi_E = \oint_S \mathbf E \cdot d \mathbf S = E \cdot (4 \pi r^2)
$$

Hence,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0} \implies \mathbf E = \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r^2} \mathbf{\hat r}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf r}$ points radially outward for $Q > 0$ and radially inward for $Q < 0$.

</div>

### Solid Sphere

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E =
        \begin{cases}
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Qr}{R^3} \mathbf{\hat r} & , & r < R \\[2ex]
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf r} & , & r \geq R
        \end{cases}}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed in the volume.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a uniformly charged solid sphere of radius $R$ and volume charge density $\rho$. Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$.

By symmetry:

- $\mathbf E$ is radial.
- $E$ is constant over the Gaussian sphere.

Flux through the sphere:

$$
    \Phi_E = \oint_S \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S = E \cdot (4 \pi r^2)
$$

Apply Gauss's law:

$$
    \Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Hence,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

The derivation splits into two regions.

**Inside the sphere $(r < R)$:**

The enclosed charge is

$$
    Q = \rho \cdot \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \right)
$$

Using Gauss's law,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{\rho \cdot \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi r^3 \right)}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\rho r}{3 \varepsilon_0}
$$

**Outside the sphere $(r \geq R)$:**

The enclosed charge is the total charge

$$
    Q = \rho \cdot \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)
$$

Thus,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{\rho \left( \frac{4}{3} \pi R^3 \right)}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\rho R^3}{3 \varepsilon_0 r^2}
$$

which can also be written as

$$
    E=\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}
$$

showing that, outside, the sphere behaves like a point charge located at its center.

</div>

### Spherical Shell

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E =
        \begin{cases}
            \, \mathbf 0 & , & r < R \\[2ex]
            \dfrac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \dfrac{Q}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf r} & , & r \geq R
        \end{cases}}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed on the surface.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a uniformly charged spherical shell of radius $R$ with surface charge density $\sigma$. Choose a spherical Gaussian surface of radius $r$.

By symmetry:

- $\mathbf E$ is radial.
- $E$ is constant over the Gaussian sphere.

Apply Gauss's law:

$$
    \Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Hence,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

The derivation splits into two regions.

**Inside the shell $(r < R)$:**

No charge is enclosed:

$$
    Q = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = 0 \implies E = 0
$$

**Outside the shell $(r \geq R)$:**

The enclosed charge is

$$
    Q = \sigma \cdot (4 \pi R^2)
$$

Thus,

$$
    E \cdot (4 \pi r^2) = \frac{\sigma \cdot (4 \pi R^2)}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E=\frac{\sigma R^2}{\varepsilon_0 r^2}
$$

which may also be written as

$$
    E=\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{r^2}
$$

Thus, outside the shell, the field is the same as that of a point charge at the center, while inside the shell the electric field is identically zero.

</div>

### Infinite Line

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E = \frac{1}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\lambda}{r} \hat{\mathbf r}}
$$

where $\lambda$ is the uniform linear charge density.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Choose a cylindrical Gaussian surface of radius $r$ and length $L$, coaxial with the infinite line charge of linear charge density $\lambda$.

By symmetry:

- $\mathbf E$ is radial.
- $E$ is constant over the curved surface.
- $\mathbf E \perp \mathrm d \mathbf S$ on the curved surface.
- $\mathbf E \parallel \mathrm d \mathbf S$ on the end caps, so their flux is zero.

Flux through the cylinder:

$$
    \Phi_E = \oint_S \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S = E \cdot (2 \pi r L)
$$

Enclosed charge:

$$
    Q = \lambda L.
$$

Apply Gauss's law:

$$
    \Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Hence,

$$
    E \cdot (2 \pi r L) = \frac{\lambda L}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E = \frac{\lambda}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0 r}
$$

Therefore, the electric field is

$$
    \mathbf E = \frac{1}{2 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\lambda}{r} \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is directed radially outward for $\lambda > 0$ and inward for $\lambda < 0$.

</div>

### Infinite Plane
    
$$
    \boxed{\mathbf E = \frac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \hat{\mathbf r}}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the uniform surface charge density.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Choose a cylindrical pillbox Gaussian surface of cross-sectional area $A$, with its flat faces parallel to the infinite plane carrying uniform surface charge density $\sigma$.

By symmetry:

- $\mathbf E$ is perpendicular to the plane.
- $E$ has the same magnitude on both sides.
- Flux through the curved surface is zero.

Flux through the pillbox:

$$
    \Phi_E = \oint_S \mathbf E \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf S = EA + EA = 2 EA
$$

Enclosed charge:

$$
    Q = \sigma A
$$

Apply Gauss's law:

$$
    \Phi_E = \frac{Q}{\varepsilon_0}
$$

Hence,

$$
    2EA = \frac{\sigma A}{\varepsilon_0} \implies E=\frac{\sigma}{2\varepsilon_0}
$$

Therefore, the electric field is

$$
    \mathbf E = \frac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf n}$ is the unit normal to the plane. The field points away from a positively charged plane, and toward a negatively charged plane.

</div>

### Charged Ring

$$
    \mathbf E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q x}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \hat{\mathbf x} \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

where $Q$ is the total charge uniformly distributed on the ring.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a uniformly charged ring of radius $R$ carrying total charge $Q$. We wish to find the electric field at a point $P$ on the axis of the ring ($x$-axis), a distance $x$ from its center.

Every charge element $\mathrm d q$ on the ring is the same distance $r$ from $P$.

$$
    r = \sqrt{R^2 + x^2}.
$$

From Coulomb's law, the magnitude of the field due to $\mathrm d q$ is

$$
    \mathrm d E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathrm d q}{R^2 + x^2}
$$

Let $\theta$ be the angle between the line joining $\mathrm d q$ to $P$ and the axis. Then

$$
    \cos \theta = \frac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}
$$

The axial component is

$$
    \mathrm d E_x = \mathrm d E \cos \theta = \left( \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{\mathrm d q}{R^2 + x^2} \right) \left( \frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}} \right)
$$

By symmetry, for every charge element on the ring, there is an opposite element whose vertical (radial) component of electric field exactly cancels. Therefore,

- radial components cancel,
- only the axial components add.

Hence,

$$
    E = \int \mathrm d E_x = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{x}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \int \mathrm d q
$$

Since

$$
    \int \mathrm d q = Q
$$

we obtain

$$
    \mathbf E(x) = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q x}{(R^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} \hat{\mathbf x}
$$

---

At the center $x = 0$, so $\mathbf E = \mathbf 0$. Therefore, the electric field vanishes at the center.

---

Far from the ring, $x \gg R$, so

$$
    \left( R^2 + x^2 \right)^{3/2} \approx x^3
$$

Therefore,

$$
    E = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{x^2} \qquad \text{(Far from the ring)}
$$

which is exactly the electric field of a point charge $Q$.

</div>

### Charged Disc

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \left( 1 - \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}\right) \mathbf{\hat x}  \qquad \text{(On the axis)}
$$

where $\sigma$ is the uniform surface charge density.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider a uniformly charged disc of radius $R$ with surface charge density $\sigma$. We want the electric field at a point on the axis, a distance $x$ from the center.

Divide the disc into rings. Take a thin ring of radius $r$ and thickness $dr$. Its area is

$$
    \mathrm d A = 2 \pi r \, \mathrm d r
$$

Hence its charge is

$$
    dq = \sigma \, \mathrm d A = 2 \pi \sigma r \, \mathrm d r
$$

The electric field on the axis of a ring is

$$
    \mathrm d E_x = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{x \, \mathrm d q}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0} \frac{2 \pi \sigma x r \, \mathrm d r}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \frac{\sigma x}{2 \varepsilon_0} \frac{r \, \mathrm d r}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}
$$

By symmetry, only the axial $(\hat{\mathbf x})$ component survives. So,

$$
    E = \frac{\sigma x}{2 \varepsilon_0} \int_0^R \frac{r \mathrm d r}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}}
$$

Using integration by substitution (using $u = r^2 + x^2$), we get

$$
    \int_0^R \frac{r \, \mathrm d r}{(r^2 + x^2)^{3/2}} = \left[ -\frac{1}{\sqrt{r^2 + x^2}} \right]_0^R = \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{\sqrt{R^2+x^2}} \qquad (x > 0)
$$

Therefore,

$$
    \mathbf E = \dfrac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0} \left( 1 - \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{R^2 + x^2}}\right) \mathbf{\hat x}
$$

---

At the center,  $x \to 0^+$, so

$$
    E = \frac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0}
$$

This agrees with the field just outside an infinite charged sheet in the limit.

---

Far away from the disc, $x \gg R$, so, using binomial approximation gives

$$
    \sqrt{R^2 + x^2} \approx x \left( 1 + \frac{R^2}{2 x^2} \right)
$$

we get

$$
    E = \frac{\sigma R^2}{4\varepsilon_0 x^2} \qquad \text{(Far away from the disc)}
$$

Since the total charge is $Q = \sigma \pi R^2$, this becomes

$$
    E = \frac{1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0} \frac{Q}{x^2} \qquad \text{(Far away from the disc)}
$$

which is exactly the electric field of a point charge.

---

For an infinite disc, $ R \to \infty$, so

$$
    E = \frac{\sigma}{2 \varepsilon_0}
$$

which is the electric field for an infinite uniformly charged plane.

</div>
