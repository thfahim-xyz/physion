# Science

## Science

Science is a systematic discipline that builds and organizes knowledge in the form of testable explanations about nature and society through the scientific method. It is the systematic pursuit of knowledge through observation, evidence, logical reasoning, and methods that allow claims to be tested, criticized, and improved. The word *science* comes from the Latin word *scientia* which means 'knowledge, awareness, understanding'.

Modern science is typically divided into 3 major branches:

- **Natural Sciences:** Natural sciences is a branch of science concerned with the description, understanding, and prediction of natural phenomena, based on empirical evidence from observation and experimentation. Most people often use the word *science* to refer specifically to the natural sciences. Physics is the most fundamental of the natural sciences, based on the fact that it studies the most basic constituents and laws from which other natural sciences emerge. Other subdivisions are astronomy, chemistry, biochemistry, biology, microbiology, Earth science, etc.

- **Social Sciences:** Social sciences is a branch of science devoted to the study of societies and the relationships among members within those societies. It includes anthropology, archaeology, economics, geography, history, linguistics, psychology, sociology, political science, etc.

- **Formal Sciences:** Formal sciences is a branch of science studying disciplines concerned with abstract structures described by formal systems. The consideration of the formal sciences as a branch of science is often debated due to the non-empirical and axiomatic nature of the disciplines within it. Major subbranches include logic, mathematics, statistics, computer science, artificial intelligence, game theory, data science, information theory, crytography, etc.

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“[Science] works. It is not perfect. It can be misused. It is only a tool. But it is by far the best tool we have, self-correcting, ongoing, applicable to everything. It has two rules. First: there are no sacred truths; all assumptions must be critically examined; arguments from authority are worthless. Second: whatever is inconsistent with the facts must be discarded or revised. We must understand the Cosmos as it is and not confuse how it is with how we wish it to be. The obvious is sometimes false; the unexpected is sometimes true.”</p>

    <cite>― Carl Sagan</cite>

</blockquote>

## Scientific Method

The **scientific method** is a systematic approach to understanding nature through observation, hypothesis formation, prediction, experimentation, and revision. It is not a rigid sequence of steps; rather, it is an **iterative process** in which ideas are continually tested against evidence.

$$
\boxed{
\text{Observation}
\rightarrow
\text{Hypothesization}
\rightarrow
\text{Prediction}
\rightarrow
\text{Experimentation}
\rightarrow
\text{Revision}
\rightarrow
\text{New Observation}
\rightarrow \cdots
}
$$

This continual confrontation between **ideas and evidence** is what allows scientific knowledge to become increasingly reliable.

### Observation

**Observation** is the systematic collection of information about a phenomenon. Observations may be qualitative, such as noticing that an object falls toward the ground, or quantitative, such as measuring its acceleration.

Observation provides the empirical basis from which questions can be formulated and explanations can be sought.

### Hypothesization

A **hypothesis** is a tentative, testable explanation for an observed phenomenon. It should be formulated precisely enough that its consequences can be investigated experimentally.

A good hypothesis does more than merely describe what has been observed; it provides an explanation that can lead to **testable predictions**.

### Prediction

A **prediction** is a specific consequence that should be observed if a hypothesis is correct.

Predictions are derived from the hypothesis and, when possible, expressed quantitatively. A hypothesis becomes scientifically useful when it makes predictions that could potentially be shown to disagree with observation.

### Experimentation

**Experimentation** involves deliberately testing predictions by making controlled observations or measurements.

An experiment should be designed so that its results can distinguish between competing explanations. The experimental results are then compared with the predictions of the hypothesis.

If the observations disagree with a prediction, the hypothesis is challenged. If they agree, the hypothesis gains empirical support, although it is not thereby proven to be absolutely true.

### Revision

Scientific knowledge is **revised** when new evidence reveals limitations, errors, or previously unknown conditions.

If experimental results disagree with predictions, the hypothesis may be modified, rejected, or replaced. Even when a hypothesis successfully explains existing observations, new observations may reveal a broader theory that contains it as a special case.

## Scientific Knowledge

Scientific knowledge consists of observations, measurements, concepts, models, hypotheses, laws, theories, and other forms of justified understanding about the natural world.

These terms are sometimes used interchangeably in everyday language, but they have distinct meanings in science. In particular, a **scientific theory does not become a law after enough evidence**, and **a law is not necessarily more certain or fundamental than a theory**. They serve different purposes.

Ultimately, scientific knowledge is not a collection of permanently unquestionable truths. It is a **structured, evidence-based, self-correcting body of knowledge** whose claims differ in their purpose, scope, evidential support, and logical status.

### Fact

A **scientific fact** is an observation or empirical result that is so well established by evidence that it is accepted as reliably true within the relevant conditions.

For example, that objects near Earth's surface accelerate downward is an empirical fact.

Scientific facts are not necessarily absolutely certain. Because scientific knowledge is fallible, even well-established facts can be refined when better measurements or broader circumstances are discovered.

### Evidence

**Evidence** is empirical information that supports, challenges, or helps distinguish between scientific claims.

Evidence may come from direct observation, measurement, experiment, or systematic analysis of natural phenomena.

For example, measurements of planetary motion provide evidence relevant to theories of gravitation.

Evidence does not automatically establish a conclusion. Its significance depends on its reliability, the method by which it was obtained, and how well competing explanations account for it.

### Hypothesis

A **hypothesis** is a tentative, testable explanation or proposition about a phenomenon.

A scientific hypothesis should lead to consequences that can be compared with observations. It may gain support through successful testing, or it may be modified or rejected when evidence conflicts with its predictions.

A hypothesis is therefore not simply an educated guess; it is a **testable explanatory claim**.

### Model

A **scientific model** is a simplified representation of a system, object, process, or phenomenon used to understand, explain, or predict its behavior.

Models deliberately omit some aspects of reality in order to focus on those that are relevant to a particular problem.

Examples include:

- the ideal gas model,
- the Bohr model of the atom,
- the particle model of light,
- the simple harmonic oscillator,
- the standard model of particle physics.

A model does not need to reproduce every detail of reality to be useful. Its usefulness depends on how accurately it represents the aspects of a system relevant to the question being investigated.

### Law

A **scientific law** describes a regular pattern or relationship observed in nature.

Laws are often expressed mathematically because mathematics allows the relationship to be stated precisely.

A law generally tells us **what happens**, rather than providing a complete explanation of **why it happens**.

**A scientific law does not become a theory after accumulating enough evidence**. Laws and theories have different roles: laws describe regularities, whereas theories provide explanatory frameworks.

### Principle

A **scientific principle** is a broad foundational statement or rule used to organize, constrain, or explain scientific phenomena.

Examples include:

- the principle of conservation of energy,
- the principle of relativity,
- the Pauli exclusion principle,
- the equivalence principle.

The precise meaning of *principle* varies between fields. It is often used for a foundational idea from which other results can be derived or against which theories and models can be evaluated.

### Theory

A **scientific theory** is a comprehensive, well-supported explanatory framework that accounts for a large body of observations and experimental results.

A theory typically consists of interconnected concepts, principles, mathematical relationships, models, and mechanisms that explain why and how phenomena occur.

Examples include:

- the theory of evolution,
- the theory of electromagnetism,
- the theory of relativity,
- quantum theory,
- the kinetic theory of gases

In science, *theory* does **not** mean a mere speculation or guess; *theory* does **not** become a law or fact after being tested. A scientific theory is supported by substantial evidence and has survived repeated attempts to test its predictions.

Nevertheless, a theory remains open to refinement or replacement when new evidence or a more successful framework becomes available.

### Theorem

A **theorem** is a proposition that has been **logically derived from previously established assumptions, definitions, axioms, or propositions**.

The term is primarily used in mathematics and formal systems rather than empirical science.

A theorem is established by **proof**, not by experiment.

Scientific theories can contain mathematical theorems, but a theorem itself does not establish an empirical fact about the physical world. Its applicability to nature depends on whether the assumptions and mathematical model correspond adequately to physical reality.

### Proof

A **proof** is a logically valid argument demonstrating that a conclusion follows necessarily from a set of premises, definitions, or axioms.

Proof is fundamental to mathematics and formal logic.

In empirical science, however, the word *proof* should be used carefully. Experiments can provide evidence for a scientific claim, but they generally cannot establish an empirical hypothesis with the same deductive certainty as a mathematical theorem.

For this reason, scientists usually speak of **evidence, confirmation, support, or corroboration** rather than absolute experimental proof.

For example, repeated experiments can strongly support the ideal gas law, but they do not logically prove that the equation must hold under every conceivable condition.

### Explanation

A scientific **explanation** describes how or why a phenomenon occurs by relating it to underlying mechanisms, principles, laws, or theoretical structures.

For example, the theory of evolution explains biological diversity through mechanisms including variation, inheritance, and natural selection.

Explanation goes beyond merely describing a regularity: it attempts to identify the **mechanisms and relationships responsible for it**.

### Prediction

A **prediction** is a statement about what should be observed if a particular hypothesis, model, or theory is correct.

Predictions may concern previously unobserved phenomena or future measurements.

For example, general relativity predicted that light would be deflected by gravity. Such predictions allow theories to be subjected to empirical tests.

### Corroboration

**Corroboration** refers to the degree to which a scientific claim continues to withstand serious attempts to test and challenge it.

A hypothesis that successfully predicts many independent observations becomes increasingly well supported.

However, successful tests do not establish absolute certainty. A future experiment may reveal conditions under which the hypothesis fails.

### Falsification

**Falsification** is the possibility of showing that a scientific claim is inconsistent with empirical evidence.

A claim is scientifically testable when there are conceivable observations that could count against it.

For example, if a theory predicts that a particular quantity must have a specific value under specified conditions, an accurately measured contradictory result could falsify that prediction and potentially require revision of the theory.

Falsification is therefore an important aspect of scientific criticism, although in practice scientists rarely abandon an entire theory because of a single anomalous result. Experimental errors, auxiliary assumptions, and measurement limitations must also be considered.

## Philosophy of Science

The **philosophy of science** examines the foundations, methods, limits, and implications of scientific knowledge. It asks questions such as: What makes a belief scientific? How do we justify scientific theories? What is the role of observation and reasoning? And do scientific theories describe reality itself, or merely predict observations?

Several philosophical principles are particularly important for understanding the scientific worldview. Together, they support a worldview in which our understanding of nature is built through **reason and evidence, continually subjected to criticism, and always open to revision**.

### Fallibilism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“Ignorance more frequently begets confidence than does knowledge: it is those who know little, not those who know much, who so positively assert that this or that problem will never be solved by science.”</p>

    <cite>― Charles Darwin</cite>

</blockquote>

**Fallibilism** is the view that human knowledge is inherently **provisional and potentially mistaken**. No scientific claim is immune to revision, regardless of how well supported it currently is.

Scientific theories are therefore not regarded as absolutely certain truths. They are accepted because they have survived extensive testing and provide successful explanations and predictions. New evidence may nevertheless reveal limitations or require modification.

Fallibilism does not mean that all beliefs are equally uncertain. Some claims can be supported by overwhelming evidence and can be regarded as highly reliable while remaining, in principle, revisable.

Thus, scientific knowledge is best understood as **reliable but not infallible**.

### Skepticism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“The deepest sin against the human mind is to believe things without evidence.”</p>

    <cite>― Thomas Huxley</cite>

</blockquote>

**Skepticism** is the practice of critically questioning claims rather than accepting them merely because they appear plausible, authoritative, or familiar.

In science, skepticism motivates scientists to ask:

- What evidence supports this claim?
- Could there be an alternative explanation?
- How reliable are the measurements?
- Can the result be independently reproduced?
- What observation could show the claim to be wrong?

Scientific skepticism is therefore not the rejection of knowledge, but a method for **protecting knowledge from error**.

### Naturalism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“The cosmos is all that is or ever was or ever will be.”</p>

    <cite>― Carl Sagan</cite>

</blockquote>

**Naturalism** is the methodological principle that scientific explanations should appeal to **natural processes and entities** that can be investigated empirically.

For example, physics explains the motion of objects through concepts such as force, momentum, and energy, while biology explains biological phenomena through mechanisms such as evolution, genetics, and natural selection.

This principle is usually called **methodological naturalism**. It does not necessarily assert that nothing exists beyond the natural world (and certainly does not assert that something exists beyond the natural world); rather, it establishes a methodological boundary for scientific investigation. 

Science asks how phenomena can be explained through mechanisms that can be investigated and tested.

### Empiricism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“It doesn't matter how beautiful your theory is, it doesn't matter how smart you are. If it doesn't agree with experiment, it's wrong.”</p>

    <cite>― Richard Feynman</cite>

</blockquote>

**Empiricism** emphasizes the role of **observation and experience** in acquiring knowledge about the world.

Scientific claims ultimately have to be connected to empirical evidence. Measurements, observations, and experiments allow us to determine whether our descriptions of nature agree with what actually occurs.

Empiricism therefore provides the **evidential foundation** of science: our theories must ultimately confront experience.

However, science is not purely empirical. Observations alone do not produce theories; they must be interpreted using concepts, mathematical models, and logical reasoning.

### Rationalism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“The book of nature is written in the language of mathematics.”</p>

    <cite>― Galileo Galilei</cite>

</blockquote>

**Rationalism** emphasizes the role of **reason and logical inference** in acquiring and organizing knowledge.

Science depends heavily on rational reasoning. From hypotheses and established principles, scientists derive consequences that can be compared with observations.

For example, from Newton's laws and a model of a physical system, we can mathematically derive its predicted motion.

Mathematics is particularly important because it allows scientific theories to express relationships precisely and generate quantitative predictions.

Nevertheless, scientific rationalism is constrained by empirical evidence. A logically consistent theory is not necessarily a correct description of nature. Its predictions must still agree with observation.

Thus, science combines **reason to construct and analyze theories** with **evidence to test them**.

### Realism

<blockquote>
    
    <p>“The good thing about science is that it's true whether or not you believe in it.”</p>

    <cite>― Neil deGrasse Tyson</cite>

</blockquote>

**Scientific realism** is the philosophical position that the entities and structures described by successful scientific theories are, at least approximately, **real features of the world**, even when they cannot be directly observed.

For example, a scientific realist may regard electrons, atoms, electromagnetic fields, and spacetime as genuinely existing aspects of physical reality rather than merely useful mathematical concepts.

Realism is closely connected with the remarkable success of science: theories often allow us to predict phenomena, manipulate previously unobservable entities, and develop technologies based on their assumed properties.

However, scientific realism does not require that every current theory be completely correct. Scientific theories can be approximately true and later replaced by deeper theories.

For example, classical mechanics remains extraordinarily successful within its domain even though it is not the most fundamental description of nature.

Scientific realism therefore maintains that the success of science is best understood, at least in part, by supposing that our theories **capture genuine aspects of an objective reality**.
