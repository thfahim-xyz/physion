# Physics

## Physics

**Physics is a branch of science that is concerned with the nature and properties of matter and energy.**

It is the study of the fundamental laws governing matter, energy, space, and time, and the interactions between them.

## Scope of Physics

The scope of scope extends from the smallest known constituents of matter to the structure and evolution of the Universe.

At the microscopic scale, physics investigates atoms, nuclei, elementary particles, and quantum fields. At macroscopic scales, it describes the behaviour of matter through mechanics, thermodynamics, electromagnetism, and fluid dynamics. At the largest scales, physics provides the foundations for understanding stars, galaxies, spacetime, and the evolution of the Universe through relativity and cosmology.

Physics also provides the theoretical foundations of many other sciences and technologies. Chemistry relies heavily on quantum mechanics and statistical physics; biology uses physical principles to understand molecular and cellular processes; and engineering applies physical laws to develop technologies and systems.

Thus, the scope of physics is not defined merely by a particular size scale or class of objects. It is unified by the search for quantitative, general laws of nature and by the mathematical frameworks used to describe them.

### Major Domains

Physics is traditionally divided into 2 branches:

- Classical Physics (where the laws of physics are deterministic)
- Quantum Physics (where the laws of physics are probabilistic)

### Major Theories

The major theories of physics are:

- **Classical Mechanics:** Studies the motion of objects.
- **Thermodynamics:** Studies heat and thermal properties of matter.
- **Statistical Mechanics:** Studies the motion of a large number of objects.
- **Electromagnetism:** Studies the nature of electricity and magnetism.
- **Theory of Relativity:** Studies the motion of objects with velocities near the speed of light.
- **Quantum Mechanics:** Studies the motion of microscopic particles.

## Fundamental Structures of Nature

Physics seeks to understand nature by identifying its most fundamental **structures, entities, interactions, and principles**. Although the theories of physics are divided into different fields, they can be viewed as different descriptions of the same underlying physical reality.

### Space and Time

Physical events occur within **space and time**.

**Space** provides the framework in which physical objects and events have position, distance, and spatial relationships. In classical physics, space is treated as an absolute three-dimensional background.

**Time** provides the ordering of events and allows us to describe change and motion. In classical mechanics, time is also treated as absolute and independent of the observer.

Classical physics treats space and time as separate, absolute backgrounds. Special relativity instead combines them into four-dimensional **spacetime**, in which measurements of space and time depend on the observer's state of motion.

General relativity goes further: spacetime is not merely a passive stage on which physics occurs. Its geometry is dynamical and is related to the distribution of matter and energy. Thus, the geometry of the universe itself becomes a physical entity.

### Fields and Interactions

A **field** is a physical quantity or entity defined throughout space and time. Instead of describing physical reality solely in terms of individual objects, modern physics describes many phenomena in terms of fields.

Fields provide a framework for describing **interactions**. Physical systems influence one another through interactions associated with fundamental fields.

The four known fundamental interactions are:

1. **Gravitational interaction**
2. **Electromagnetic interaction**
3. **Weak interaction**
4. **Strong interaction**

The electromagnetic, weak, and strong interactions are described within quantum field theory, while gravity is currently described by general relativity.

In classical physics, interactions may appear as forces acting between objects. In modern field theories, however, the field itself is often regarded as fundamental.

Quantum field theory provides an even deeper picture: what we call a **particle** can be understood as a quantized excitation of an underlying field.

### Matter and Energy

**Matter** refers broadly to physical entities that constitute material systems. At microscopic scales, ordinary matter is composed of atoms, which themselves are composed of electrons and atomic nuclei. At a more fundamental level, the Standard Model describes elementary particles such as quarks and leptons. Matter possesses properties such as mass, electric charge, momentum, and energy.

**Energy** is a conserved physical quantity associated with the state and dynamics of a system. It appears in many forms, including kinetic, potential, thermal, electromagnetic, chemical, and rest energy.

Relativity reveals a deep connection between mass and energy:

$$
    E_0=mc^2
$$

Mass therefore contributes to the total energy of a physical system.

Matter and energy are also connected to spacetime. In general relativity, the distribution of matter and energy determines the geometry of spacetime through Einstein's field equations.

At the quantum level, matter and energy are further unified through quantum fields. Consequently, the classical distinction between "matter" and "energy" is not fundamental in the simple everyday sense.

### Symmetry and Conservation Laws

**Symmetry** is one of the deepest organizing principles in physics. A physical system possesses a symmetry when its relevant properties or laws remain unchanged under a particular transformation.

Examples include spatial translation symmetry, rotational symmetry, time-translation symmetry, gauge symmetries, Lorentz symmetry.

Symmetry is deeply connected to **conservation laws** through Noether's theorem. For continuous symmetries, the theorem establishes a correspondence between symmetries of the laws of physics and conserved quantities.

Other conservation laws, such as conservation of electric charge, are associated with deeper symmetries of physical theories.

Conservation laws impose powerful constraints on physical processes. A process may be dynamically possible only if it satisfies the relevant conservation laws.

Symmetry therefore provides more than aesthetic simplicity: it helps determine the **form of the fundamental laws themselves**.

### Causality and Dynamics

**Causality** concerns the relationship between events and their possible influences. If one event causes another, the laws of physics must permit a causal connection between them.

Special relativity places a fundamental limit on causal influence: information cannot propagate through spacetime faster than the speed of light in vacuum.

**Dynamics** describes how physical systems change.

A physical theory generally consists of mathematical laws that specify how the state of a system evolves.

Classical mechanics expresses dynamics through equations such as

$$
    \mathbf F = m \mathbf a.
$$

Electromagnetism is governed by Maxwell's equations:

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf E = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}, \quad
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf B = 0, \quad
    \nabla \times \mathbf E = - \frac{\partial \mathbf B}{\partial t}, \quad
    \nabla \times \mathbf B = \mu_0 \left( \mathbf J + \varepsilon_0 \frac{\partial\mathbf E}{\partial t} \right)
$$

Quantum systems evolve according to quantum dynamical laws such as the Schrödinger equation,

$$
    i \hbar \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{H}(t) \ket{\Psi(t)}
$$

General relativity describes the dynamics of spacetime geometry through Einstein's field equations,

$$
    G_{\mu \nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu \nu} = \frac{8 \pi G}{c^4} T_{\mu \nu}
$$

The most fundamental dynamical principle that unifies all of physics is the **principle of stationary action**:

$$
    \boxed{\delta S = 0}
$$
