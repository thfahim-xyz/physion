## Position

Position is an object's location in space at a given time, relative to a defined reference frame.

An object's location in space is given by its coordinates in a coordinate system. We usually describe position using a position vector $\mathbf r \equiv \mathbf r(t)$, which points to the coordinates of the object.

In common coordinate systems, $\mathbf r$ takes the following forms:

$$
\begin{align*}
    \mathbf r & = \mathbf r (x,y,z) = x \hat{\mathbf x} + y \hat{\mathbf y} + z \hat{\mathbf z} \qquad & \text{(Cartesian coordinates)}\\
     & = \mathbf r (r, \theta, z) = r \hat{\mathbf r}(\theta) + z \hat{\mathbf z} \qquad & \text{(Cylindrical coordinates)} \\
     & = \mathbf r (r, \theta, \varphi) = r \hat{\mathbf r}(\theta, \varphi) \qquad & \text{(Spherical coordinates)}
\end{align*}
$$

### Linear Coordinates

Linear coordinates are coordinates that describe location by measuring distances along lines or from a reference point.

**Example:** In Cartesian, cylindrical and spherical coordinates, $x$, $y$, $z$ and $r$ are linear coordinates.

### Angular Coordinates

Angular coordinates are coordinates that describe location by measuring direction through angles relative to chosen reference lines, planes, or axes.

**Example:** In cylindrical and spherical coordinates, $\theta$ and $\phi$ are angular coordinates.

## Displacement

Displacement is the change in position of an object.

$$
    \boxed{\Delta \mathbf r = \int \mathrm d \mathbf r = \mathbf r(t) - \mathbf r(t_0)}
$$

where $\mathrm d \mathbf r$ is the infinitesimal displacement.

### Distance

Distance is the total amount of path traveled by an object in space.

Distance is described by arc length $s$, given by

$$
    \boxed{s = \int \mathrm d s}
$$

where $\mathrm d s$ is the infinitesimal distance (arc length), which is defined as

$$
    \mathrm d s = |\mathrm d \mathbf r|
$$

## Velocity

Velocity is the rate of change of position with respect to time.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v = \dot{\mathbf r}}
$$

The magnitude of velocity is called speed.

### Linear Velocity

Linear velocity is the rate of change of a linear coordinate with respect to time.

Radial velocity is an type of linear velocity.

$$
    \mathbf v_r = \dot r \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

It is associated with translational motion.

### Angular Velocity

Angular velocity is the rate of change of an angular coordinate with respect to time.

$$
    \boxed{\boldsymbol \omega = \dot{\theta} \hat{\mathbf n}}
$$

It is associated with rotational or orbital motion.

## Acceleration

Rate of change of velocity with respect to time.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf a = \dot{\mathbf v} = \ddot{\mathbf r}}
$$

### Linear Acceleration

Rate of change of linear velocity with respect to time.

Radial acceleration is a type of linear acceleration

$$
    \mathbf a_r = \dot{\mathbf v}_r = \ddot r \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

### Angular Acceleration

Rate of change of angular velocity with respect to time.

$$
    \boxed{\boldsymbol \alpha = \dot{\boldsymbol \omega} = \ddot{\theta} \hat{\mathbf n}}
$$

## Moment of Inertia

Moment of inertia is a measure of an object’s resistance to changes in its rotational motion about a given axis.

For discrete masses,

$$
    \boxed{I = \sum mr^2}
$$

For continuous mass distributions,

$$
    \boxed{I = \int r^2 \, \mathrm d m}
$$

**Examples:**

- Thin Rod: 

    $$
        I = \frac{1}{12}ML^2 \qquad \text{(About the perpendicular axis through the center)}
    $$

- Thin Plate: 

    $$
        I = \frac{1}{12}M(a^2 + b^2) \qquad \text{(About the perpendicular axis through the center)}
    $$

- Solid Sphere: 

    $$
        I = \frac{2}{3}MR^2 \qquad \text{(About any diameter)}
    $$

- Spherical Shell:

    $$
        I = \frac{2}{5}MR^2 \qquad \text{(About any diameter)}
    $$

- Solid Cylinder: 

    $$
        I = \frac{1}{2}MR^2 \qquad \text{(About the central symmetry axis)}
    $$

- Thin Ring:

    $$
        I = MR^2 \qquad \text{(About the central symmetry axis)}
    $$

## Momentum

Momentum is a physical quantity that describes the motion of an object and its resistance to being stopped.

### Linear Momentum

Linear momentum is the product of an object's mass and its velocity.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf p = m \mathbf v}
$$

### Angular Momentum

Angular momentum is the quantity that measures rotational motion of an object about a point or axis.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf L = \mathbf r \times \mathbf p}
$$

It is the rotational analogue of linear momentum.

## Force

A force is an action that can cause an object to change its velocity or shape, or to resist other forces, or to cause changes of pressure in a fluid.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf F = \dot{\mathbf p}}
$$

**Examples:**

- Gravitational Force $(F_g = mg)$: Gravitational force is the force exerted by gravitational fields.
- Electric Force $(F_e = qE)$: The electric force is the force exerted by electric fields.
- Magnetic Force $(F_m = qvB \sin \theta)$: The magnetic force is the force exerted by magnetic fields.
- Normal Force $(F_n)$: The normal force is the component of a contact force that is perpendicular to the surface that an object contacts.
- Spring Force $(F_s = -k x)$: Spring force is the restoring force produced when a spring or spring-like object is stretched or compressed.
- Linear Drag Force $(F_d = -bv)$: Linear drag force is the force that resists motion through a fluid and is proportional to velocity.
- Quadratic Drag Force $(F_D = \frac{1}{2} \rho v^2 C_D A)$: Quadratic drag force is the force that resists motion through a fluid and is proportional to the square of speed.
- Dry Friction $(f)$: Dry friction is the friction force between two solid surfaces in contact, without lubrication. Given by

$$
    f =
    \begin{cases}
        F, & F \leq \mu_s F_n \\
        \mu_k F_n, & F > \mu_s F_n
    \end{cases}
$$

### Conservative Force

A force $\mathbf F$ is conservative if there exists a scalar potential energy function $\phi$ such that

$$
    \mathbf F = - \nabla \phi
$$

The properties of a conservative force are:

- **Mechanical Energy Conservation:** A conservative force conserves mechanical energy.

    $$
        E = K + U = \text{constant}
    $$

- **Path Independence:** The work done by a conservative force is path independent.

    $$
        \int_{C_1} \mathbf F \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r = \int_{C_2} \mathbf F \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r
    $$

- **Zero Work in a Loop:** If the conservative force does work in a closed loop, the total work done is zero.

    $$
        W = \oint \mathbf F \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r
    $$

- **Irrotational:** The curl of the conservative force field is zero.

    $$
        \nabla \times \mathbf F = \mathbf 0
    $$

### Centripetal Force

Centripetal force is the net force directed toward the center of a curved path that causes an object to follow that path.

$$
    \mathbf F_c = -m \omega^2 r \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

Centripetal force is the radial inward component of the net force acting on an object, which causes it to follow a curved path. It is not a separate force, but the name given to the net inward force required for curved motion.

### Central Force

A central force is a force that is directed towards or away from a point.

$$
    \mathbf F \equiv \mathbf F (\mathbf r) = F \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

An object moving under the influence of any central force always conserves angular momentum. Central force is conservative if and only if it is spherically symmetric (or rotationally invariant).


## Torque

Torque is the rotational effect produced by a force applied at a distance from a pivot. It is the rotational analogue of force.

$$
    \boxed{\boldsymbol \tau = \mathbf r \times \mathbf F}
$$

Torque is also the rate of change of angular momentum with respect to time.

$$
    \boldsymbol \tau = \dot{\mathbf L}
$$

## Work

In general, work is the change in energy of any physical system.

$$
    \boxed{W = \Delta E}
$$

Work is the energy transferred to or from an object via the application of force along a displacement.

$$
    \boxed{W = \int_C \mathbf F \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r}
$$

When the force is constant, work is

$$
    W = \mathbf F \cdot \mathbf s
$$

Work done against a force field $(W_{ON})$ gives energy to the system. Work done by a force field $(W_{BY})$ takes away energy from the system.

$$
    W_{ON} = -W_{BY}
$$

## Energy

Energy is the fundamental capacity of an object or a system to do work or to cause change.

Energy can exist in various forms.

### Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the form of energy that an object possesses due to its motion.

$$
    \boxed{K = \dfrac{p^2}{2m} = \dfrac{1}{2} m v^2}
$$

### Potential Energy

Potential energy is the stored energy an object or system possesses due to its position, arrangement, or state.

It is not possible to directly measure the potential energy of a system in an absolute sense, since potential energy is a relative quantity defined by an arbitrary reference point. Only changes in potential energy $(\Delta U)$  are physically meaningful and measurable.

$$
    \boxed{\Delta U = -W}
$$

Potential energy only exists if the force is conservative. Every conservative force has potential energy and a potential energy function associated with it.

$$
    \mathbf F = -\nabla U
$$

Work done against a force field $(W_{ON})$ increases the potential energy of the system (i.e., gives energy to the system). Work done by a force field $(W_{BY})$ decreases the potential energy of the system (i.e., takes away energy from the system).

$$
\begin{gather*}
    W_{ON} \implies U \; \text{increases} \\
    W_{BY} \implies U \; \text{decreases}
\end{gather*}
$$

By following two principles, one can consistently assign a non-relative value to $U$, in which case, $\Delta U$ becomes $U(\mathbf r)$.

- Wherever $F = 0$, $U \equiv 0$ as well.

- Whenever $F$ does work, potential energy is lost $(\Delta U < 0)$.

**Examples:**
- Gravitational Potential Energy $(U_g)$
- Electric Potential Energy $(U_e)$
- Elastic Potential Energy $(U_s)$

## Power

Power is the rate at which work is done or energy is transferred.

$$
    \boxed{P = \dot E}
$$
