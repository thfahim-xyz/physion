# Particle Kinematics

## Motion in One Dimension

### Horizontal Motion

Assume a particle is in motion on the $x$-axis with constant acceleration $ a $. The particle started moving from the initial position $ x_0 $ at an initial velocity $ v_0 $.

The position of the particle at time $ t $ is given by

$$
    x = x_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2 = x_0 + \frac{1}{2} (v_0 + v)t
$$

The velocity of the particle at time $ t $ is given by

$$
    v = v_0 + at
$$

The velocity at position $ x $ is given by

$$
    v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(x - x_0)
$$

### Vertical Motion

Assume a particle is in motion on the $y$-axis with constant acceleration $a$. The particle was thrown upward at a velocity $ v_0 $ from vertical position (height) $ y_0 $. The constant acceleration is usually assumed to be the acceleration due to gravity (i.e. $ a = -g $)

The vertical position of the particle at time $ t $ is given by

$$
    y = y_0 + v_0 t + \frac{1}{2} a t^2 = y_0 + \frac{1}{2} (v_0 + v)t
$$

The velocity of the particle at time $ t $ is given by

$$
    v = v_0 + a t
$$

The velocity at vertical position $ y $ is given by

$$
    v^2 = v_0^2 + 2a(y - y_0)
$$

## Motion in Two and Three Dimensions

### Projectile Motion

$$
    T = \frac{2 v_0 \sin \theta}{g}
$$

$$
    H = \frac{v_0^2 \sin^2 \theta}{2g}
$$

$$
    R = \frac{v_0^2 \sin (2 \theta)}{g}
$$

$$
    y = x \tan \theta - \frac{g x^2}{2 v_0^2 \cos^2 \theta}
$$

### Circular Motion

$$
    a_c = \frac{v^2}{r} = \omega^2 r
$$

## Relative Motion

Relative motion describes the motion of an object as observed from a reference frame that may itself be moving relative to another frame. The position, velocity, and acceleration of an object are therefore frame-dependent quantities.

For two particles $A$ and $B$, the position of $A$ relative to $B$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
    & \mathbf r_{A/B} = \mathbf r_A-\mathbf r_B \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \mathbf v_{A/B} = \mathbf v_A - \mathbf v_B \\[0.4em]
    \implies & \mathbf a_{A/B} = \mathbf a_A - \mathbf a_B
\end{aligned}
$$

An object may be at rest in one reference frame while moving in another. Relative velocity is therefore not an intrinsic property of an object; it depends on the observer's frame of reference.

### Galilean Relativity

**Galilean relativity** is the principle that the laws of classical mechanics have the same form in all inertial reference frames.

Consider two inertial frames $S$ and $S'$ moving with constant relative velocity $\mathbf V$. Let their origins coincide at $t=0$. The position vectors of an event are related by the **Galilean transformation**

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf r' = \mathbf r -\mathbf Vt, \quad t'=t}
$$

where $\mathbf V$ is the velocity of $S'$ relative to $S$.

The transformation implies that **time is absolute** in Galilean mechanics.

The velocity is given by

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v'=\mathbf v-\mathbf V} \implies \mathbf v = \mathbf v' + \mathbf V
$$

This is the classical **velocity-addition rule**.

The acceleration is given by

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf a'=\mathbf a}
$$

Acceleration is therefore invariant under Galilean transformations.

Furthermore, the relative velocity between two objects is the same in all Galilean inertial frames.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v'_{A/B} = \mathbf v_{A/B}}
$$ 

**Limitations:** Galilean relativity is valid within Newtonian mechanics, where time and space are treated as absolute and velocities combine according to

$$
\mathbf v'=\mathbf v-\mathbf V.
$$

It fails when velocities become comparable to the speed of light. Galilean relativity is the low-speed limit of relativistic kinematics $(V \ll c)$.

## Kinematics in Common Coordinate Systems

The position of a particle is described by the position vector $ \mathbf r $. The velocity and acceleration of the particle is defined, in terms of the position vector, as $ \mathbf v = \dot{\mathbf r} $ and $ a = \ddot{\mathbf r} $.

### Cartesian Coordinates

| Kinematic Variable | Equation |
|---|---|
| Position | $$\mathbf r (x, y, z) = x \, \hat{\mathbf x} + y \, \hat{\mathbf y} + z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |
| Velocity | $$\mathbf v = \dot x \, \hat{\mathbf x} + \dot y \, \hat{\mathbf y} + \dot z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |
| Acceleration | $$\mathbf a = \ddot x \, \hat{\mathbf x} + \ddot y \, \hat{\mathbf y} + \ddot z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |

### Polar Coordinates

| Kinematic Variable | Equation |
|---|---|
| Position | $$\mathbf r (r, \theta) = r \, \hat{\mathbf r}$$ |
| Velocity | $$\mathbf v = \dot r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \dot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}$$ |
| Acceleration | $$\mathbf a = \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \left( 2 \dot r \dot \theta + r \ddot \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}$$ |

For velocities,
- Radial velocity $ \mathbf v_r =  \dot r \, \hat{\mathbf r} $
- Tangential velocity $ \mathbf v_t =  r \dot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf r $

For accelerations,
- Radial acceleration $ \mathbf a_r = \ddot{r} \, \hat{\mathbf r} $
- Centripetal acceleration $ \mathbf a_c = - r \dot \theta^2 \, \hat{\mathbf r} = - \omega^2 \mathbf r = \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf v_t $
- Tangential acceleration $ \mathbf a_t = r \ddot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = \boldsymbol \alpha \times \mathbf r $
- Coriolis acceleration $ \mathbf a_{cor} = 2 \dot{r} \dot{\theta} \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = 2 \boldsymbol \omega \times \mathbf v_r $

### Cylindrical Coordinates

| Kinematic Variable | Equation |
|---|---|
| Position | $$\mathbf r (r, \theta, z) = r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |
| Velocity | $$\mathbf v = \dot r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \dot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \dot z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |
| Acceleration | $$\mathbf a = \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \left( 2 \dot r \dot \theta + r \ddot \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \ddot z \, \hat{\mathbf z}$$ |

### Spherical Coordinates

| Kinematic Variable | Equation |
|---|---|
| Position | $$\mathbf r (r, \theta, \varphi) = r \, \hat{\mathbf r}$$ |
| Velocity | $$\mathbf v = \dot r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \dot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + r \dot \varphi \sin \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}$$ |
| Acceleration | $$\mathbf a = \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 - r \dot{\varphi}^2 \sin^2 \theta \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \left( r \ddot \theta + 2 \dot r \dot \theta - r \dot{\varphi}^2 \sin \theta \cos \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \left( r \ddot \varphi \sin \theta + 2 \dot r \dot \varphi \sin \theta + 2r \dot \theta \dot \varphi \cos \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}$$ |
