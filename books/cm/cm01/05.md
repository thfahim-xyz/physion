# Particle Dynamics

## Variable Forces

A variable force depends explicitly on position, velocity, or time: $\mathbf F=\mathbf F(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,t)$. The equation of motion remains Newton's second law, stated first:

$$
    \boxed{m\frac{\mathrm d\mathbf v}{\mathrm d t}=\mathbf F(\mathbf r,\mathbf v,t)}
$$

Hence $m\,\mathrm d\mathbf v=\mathbf F\,\mathrm d t$ and $m\,\mathbf v\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf v=\mathbf F\cdot\mathrm d\mathbf r$. Integration gives $\mathbf v(t)$ and $\mathbf r(t)$ once $\mathbf F$ and initial conditions are specified.

### Space-varying Forces

**Definition:** $\mathbf F=\mathbf F(\mathbf r)$, no explicit $t$ or $\mathbf v$ dependence.

**Result:** in one dimension,

$$
    \boxed{mv\frac{\mathrm d v}{\mathrm d x}=F(x)}\implies \frac12m(v^2-v_0^2)=\int_{x_0}^{x}F(x')\,\mathrm d x'
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

by the chain rule $a=\mathrm d v/\mathrm d t=(\mathrm d v/\mathrm d x)(\mathrm d x/\mathrm d t)=v\,\mathrm d v/\mathrm d x$. Thus $m v\,\mathrm d v=F(x)\,\mathrm d x$, and integration gives the result. This is the differential form of the work-energy principle.

</div>

**Remark:** the integral supplies $v(x)$ directly; $x(t)$ then follows from $\mathrm d t=\mathrm d x/v(x)$.

### Time-varying Forces

**Definition:** $\mathbf F=\mathbf F(t)$, no explicit $\mathbf r$ or $\mathbf v$ dependence.

**Result:** in one dimension,

$$
    \boxed{m\frac{\mathrm d v}{\mathrm d t}=F(t)}\implies v(t)=v_0+\frac1m\int_{t_0}^{t}F(t')\,\mathrm d t'
$$

$$
    \implies x(t)=x_0+\int_{t_0}^{t}v(t')\,\mathrm d t'
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

direct time integration of $m\,\mathrm d v=F(t)\,\mathrm d t$, then integration of $v=\mathrm d x/\mathrm d t$.

</div>

**Remark:** velocity-dependent forces, e.g. drag $-bv$, require solving a differential equation in $v(t)$ rather than quadrature in $x$ or $t$ alone.

## Friction

**Definition:** contact force parallel to the interface opposing relative motion or impending motion. The Coulomb laws are already stated in Dynamic Quantities and are not re-derived: $f_s\le\mu_sF_n$ and $f_k=\mu_kF_n$.

The canonical consequence is the slip threshold on an incline of angle $\theta$. Resolving $m\mathbf g$ gives downslope $mg\sin\theta$ and normal $mg\cos\theta$. Static equilibrium requires $f_s=mg\sin\theta\le\mu_smg\cos\theta$, so motion impends when:

$$
    \boxed{\tan\theta_c=\mu_s}
$$

**Interpretation:** $\mu_s$ is the tangent of the angle of repose. Beyond $\theta_c$ the block slides and kinetic friction governs the acceleration.

**Limitation:** $\mu_s,\mu_k$ are empirical; they hide microscopic contact physics.

## Centripetal Force

**Definition:** the inward radial component of net force sustaining curved motion, not a separate species of force:

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf F_c=-m\omega^2r\,\hat{\mathbf r}=-m\frac{v^2}{r}\,\hat{\mathbf r}}
$$

It is supplied by tension, normal force, gravity, friction, or a combination thereof. The applications below identify the supplier in each canonical geometry.

## Inclined Planes

**Setup:** particle of mass $m$ on a smooth plane inclined at $\theta$ to the horizontal. Take $x$ downslope, $y$ normal to the plane.

**Result:** the normal force and downslope acceleration are:

$$
    \boxed{F_n=mg\cos\theta},\quad \boxed{a=g\sin\theta}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

$m\mathbf g$ resolves into $mg\sin\theta$ along $+x$ and $-mg\cos\theta$ along $y$. With no motion off the plane, $F_n-mg\cos\theta=0$, and $m\ddot x=mg\sin\theta$.

</div>

**Remark:** with kinetic friction, $\ddot x=g(\sin\theta-\mu_k\cos\theta)$; this is the only friction-incline formula needed here, the threshold being already given above.

## Connected Bodies

**Assumption:** massless, inextensible string and massless, frictionless pulley, so tension $T$ is uniform and displacements satisfy a single constraint.

### Pulleys

The canonical system is the Atwood machine: masses $m_1,m_2$ over an ideal pulley. Let $x_1+x_2=\text{constant}$, so $\ddot x_1=-\ddot x_2\equiv a$ with $a$ downward for $m_1$.

**Result:**

$$
    \boxed{a=\frac{m_1-m_2}{m_1+m_2}g},\quad \boxed{T=\frac{2m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}g}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

$m_1g-T=m_1a$ and $T-m_2g=m_2a$. Adding eliminates $T$ and gives $a$; substitution gives $T$.

</div>

**Limit:** $m_1=m_2\implies a=0$, $T=m_1g$; $m_2\to 0\implies a\to g$.

## Banked Curves

**Setup:** vehicle of mass $m$ on a curve of radius $r$ banked at $\theta$, no lateral friction (ideal design speed).

**Result:**

$$
    \boxed{\tan\theta=\frac{v^2}{rg}}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

The normal force $\mathbf F_n$ tilts inward by $\theta$. Vertically $F_n\cos\theta-mg=0$; horizontally $F_n\sin\theta=mv^2/r$. Division eliminates $F_n$ and $m$.

</div>

**Interpretation:** at this speed no friction is needed; slower or faster motion requires lateral friction.

## Conical Pendulums

**Setup:** bob of mass $m$ on a string of length $L$, sweeping a horizontal circle of radius $r=L\sin\theta$ at angular speed $\omega$, with $\theta$ the string's angle to the vertical.

**Result:**

$$
    \boxed{\tan\theta=\frac{\omega^2r}{g}},\quad \boxed{T=\frac{mg}{\cos\theta}}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

Vertically $T\cos\theta-mg=0$; horizontally $T\sin\theta=m\omega^2r$. Division gives $\tan\theta$; the vertical equation gives $T$.

</div>

**Limit:** $\omega\to 0\implies\theta\to 0$, recovering the hanging pendulum.

## Vertical Circular Motion

**Setup:** particle of mass $m$ on a string (or track) of radius $r$, moving in a vertical circle. Let $\theta$ be measured from the bottom, so height $h=r(1-\cos\theta)$.

**Result:** by energy conservation with speed $v_0$ at the bottom,

$$
    \boxed{v^2(\theta)=v_0^2-2gr(1-\cos\theta)}
$$

and, with $T$ the string tension (positive when taut) and $\theta$ measured from the bottom,

$$
    \boxed{T(\theta)=m\frac{v^2}{r}+mg\cos\theta=m\frac{v_0^2}{r}+mg(3\cos\theta-2)}
$$

so that at the bottom $(\theta=0)$ and top $(\theta=\pi)$:

$$
    \boxed{T_{\mathrm{bottom}}=m\frac{v_0^2}{r}+mg},\quad \boxed{T_{\mathrm{top}}=m\frac{v_{\mathrm{top}}^2}{r}-mg}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box_title">Derivation</div>

Tangential gravity does work $-mg\,\mathrm d h$, so $\tfrac12mv^2-\tfrac12mv_0^2=-mgh$, giving $v(\theta)$. Radially inward, tension contributes $+T$ and gravity contributes $-mg\cos\theta$, so $T-mg\cos\theta=mv^2/r$, i.e. $T=mv^2/r+mg\cos\theta$. Substitution of $v^2(\theta)$ gives the second form. At the top $(\theta=\pi)$, this is $T_{\mathrm{top}}+mg=mv_{\mathrm{top}}^2/r$.

</div>

The canonical minimum for a string (which cannot push) is $T_{\mathrm{top}}\ge 0$, giving:

$$
    \boxed{v_{\mathrm{top}}\ge\sqrt{gr}},\quad \boxed{v_0\ge\sqrt{5gr}}
$$

since $v_{\mathrm{top}}^2=v_0^2-4gr$. A rigid rod or track can support negative $T$ and complete the loop more slowly.

**Limitation:** ideal string, point mass, no air resistance.
