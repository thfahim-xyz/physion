# Keplerian Motion

## Inverse-Square Force

An inverse-square force is a central force that has the form

$$
    \boxed{F(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^2}}.
$$

For an attractive inverse-square force,

$$
    F(r) = - \frac{k}{r^2} \implies \boxed{U(r) = -\frac{k}{r}}
$$

The gravitational and electrostatic forces are inverse-square forces. The trajectories of inverse-square central forces are conic sections (e.g. circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas).

## Binet Equation for Inverse-Square Forces

For an inverse-square (attractive) force, Binet's equation is

$$
    \boxed{\frac{\mathrm d^2 u}{\mathrm d \theta^2} + u = \frac{k}{m h^2}}
$$

## Keplerian Orbit Equation

This is the solution to Binet's equation for an attractive inverse-square force. The orbit equation is

$$
    \boxed{r = \frac{a (1 - e^2)}{1 + e \cos \theta} = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos \theta}}
$$

where

- $p = a (1 - e^2) = \dfrac{L^2}{mk}$ is the semi-latus rectum

- $e = \sqrt{1 + \dfrac{2 E L^2}{m k^2}}$ is the eccentricity.

- $a$ is the semimajor axis
- $E$ is the orbital energy
- $L$ is the angular momentum.

The orbit equation describes a conic section with the force center at one focus.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The differential equation is

$$
    \frac{\mathrm d^2 u}{\mathrm d \theta^2} + u = \frac{k}{m h^2}
$$

The solution to the ODE is

$$
    u = \frac{k}{m h^2} (1 + e \cos \theta)
$$

after choosing the origin of $\theta$ appropriately.

Since $u = 1/r$, we have

$$
    r = \frac{m h^2}{k (1 + e \cos \theta)}
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{r = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos \theta}}
$$

where $p$ is the semi-latus rectum and $e$ is the eccentricity.

Thus, the solution is a conic section.

</div>

## Orbital Energy

Orbital trajectory is defined by the orbital energy:

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{r}^2 - \frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}}
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{E = \frac{m k^2}{2 L^2} (e^2 - 1)}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

For an attractive inverse-square force,

$$
    U(r) = - \frac{k}{r}
$$

Therefore,

$$
    U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = - \frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}
$$

Thus,

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot{r}^2 - \frac{k}{r} + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}}
$$

The Keplerian orbit equation is

$$
    r = \frac{a (1 - e^2)}{1 + e \cos \theta}
$$

which, when substituted into the energy equation, gives

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{m k^2}{2 L^2} (e^2 - 1)}
$$

</div>

### Types of Trajectories

The sign of the orbital energy determines whether the trajectory is bound or unbound.

**Bound Trajectory (Orbit):** If $E < 0$, the particle remains confined to a finite range of $r$. For the ideal inverse-square force, the orbit is an ellipse, with the circle as the special case $(e = 0)$.

$$
    e = 0 \implies \text{Circular orbit}
$$

$$
    0 < e < 1 \implies \text{Elliptical orbit}.
$$

**Unbound Trajectory:** If $E \geq 0$, the particle can escape to infinity. The cases are:

$$
    E = 0 \implies \text{Parabolic trajectory}
$$

$$
    E > 0 \implies \text{Hyperbolic trajectory}.
$$

In summary,

| Energy  | Eccentricity | Orbit/Trajectory |
|---------|--------------|------------------|
| $E < 0$ | $e = 0$      | Circle           |
| $E < 0$ | $0 < e < 1$  | Ellipse          |
| $E = 0$ | $e = 1$      | Parabola         |
| $E > 0$ | $e > 1$      | Hyperbola        |

## Orbital Velocity

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v = \frac{k e}{L} \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \frac{k}{L} (1 + e \cos \theta) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}}
$$

$$
    \implies v^2 = \frac{k^2}{L^2} \left( 1 + 2e \cos \theta + e^2 \right)
$$

where

- $v_r = \dfrac{k e}{L} \sin \theta$ is the radial velocity component

- $v_{\theta} = \dfrac{k}{L} (1 + e \cos \theta)$ is the transverse velocity component

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Velocity is given by

$$
    \mathbf v = \dot r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \dot \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}
$$

Using the orbit equation

$$
    r = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos \theta}
$$

we get

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v = \frac{k e}{L} \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \frac{k}{L} (1 + e \cos \theta) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}}
$$

</div>

## Turning Points

A turning point (AKA apsis) is a point where the radial velocity changes direction.

At a turning point, $\dot r = 0$.

The two common types are:

- **Periapsis:** Periapsis distance or **closest approach** is the minimum radial distance $r_p = r_{\min}$
- **Apoapsis:** Apoapsis distance or **farthest approach** is the maximum radial distance $r_a = r_{\max}$

For a bound orbit, $r_{\min} \leq r \leq r_{\max}$

For an ellipse,

$$
    \boxed{r_p = \frac{p}{1 + e} = a (1 - e)}, \quad
    \boxed{r_a = \frac{p}{1 - e} = a (1 + e)}
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{a = \frac{r_p + r_a}{2}}
$$

## Circular Orbits

**Orbit Equation:** For circular orbits, the orbit equation is

$$
    \boxed{r = r_c = \frac{L^2}{m k^2}}
$$

Therefore, a particle orbits the center of force at a constant radius $r_c$.

**Orbital Energy:** Energy is negative for circular orbits.

$$
    \boxed{E = - \frac{k}{2 r_c}}
$$

**Orbital Velocity:** Velocity is purely transverse/tangential for circular orbits.

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf v_c = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m r_c}} \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}}
$$

**Turning Points:** For circular orbits, radius is constant. So, every point in the circular orbit is a turning point.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

For a circular orbit, $e = 0$.

Let the radius for a circular orbit be $r_c$. Thus,

$$
    r = \frac{p}{1 + e \cos \theta} \implies \boxed{r_c = \frac{L^2}{m k}}
$$

$$
    E = \frac{m k^2}{2 L^2} (e^2 - 1) \implies E_c = - \frac{m k^2}{2 L^2} \implies \boxed{E = - \frac{k}{2 r_c}}
$$

$$
    \mathbf v = \frac{k e}{L} \sin \theta \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \frac{k}{L} (1 + e \cos \theta) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} \implies
    \mathbf v_c = \frac{k}{L} \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} \implies \boxed{\mathbf v_c = \sqrt{\frac{k}{m r_c}} \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}}
$$

</div>

### Stability of Circular Orbits

A circular orbit can be stable or unstable depending on the shape of the effective potential near $r_c$.

- **Stable Circular Orbit:** In a stable orbit, A small radial perturbation (or displacement) increases the effective potential and produces a restoring tendency. $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has a local minimum in stable orbits.

- **Unstable Circular Orbit:** In an unstable orbit, A small perturbation moves the particle away from the circular orbit. $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has a local maximum in unstable orbits.

Stability of an orbit can be determined using the *second-derivative test*:

$$
    \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_c} > 0 \implies \text{Stable orbit}
$$

$$
    \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_c} < 0 \implies \text{Unstable orbit}
$$

$$
    \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_c} = 0 \implies \text{Inconclusive}
$$
