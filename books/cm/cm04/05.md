# Orbital Mechanics

## Escape Orbits

An object escapes the gravitational influence of a central mass if its total orbital energy is at least zero $(E \geq 0)$. At the limiting case, $E=0$.

For an object launched from radius $r$,

$$
\frac{1}{2} m v_{\mathrm{esc}}^2 - \frac{GMm}{r} = 0
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}}
$$

The escape velocity is therefore

$$
\boxed{v_{\mathrm{esc}}=\sqrt2 v_{\rm circular}}
$$

| Launch Velocity        | Escape Trajectory |
|------------------------|-------------------|
| $v<v_{\mathrm{esc}}$   | Bound Orbit       |
| $v = v_{\mathrm{esc}}$ | Parabolic         |
| $v>v_{\mathrm{esc}}$   | Hyperbolic        |

## Satellite Motion

A satellite in orbit is continuously falling toward the central body while its tangential velocity causes it to continually miss the surface.

For a circular orbit,

$$
\boxed{v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}}
$$

For an elliptical orbit, the satellite's speed varies according to

$$
\boxed{v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right)}
$$

It moves fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis.

## Geostationary Orbits

A **geostationary satellite** is a satellite in a circular orbit that appears stationary above one point on Earth's equator. Three conditions are required:

1. The orbit must be circular.
2. The orbit must lie in Earth's equatorial plane.
3. The satellite must orbit in the same direction as Earth's rotation and have the same angular period as Earth's rotation.

For a circular orbit,

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_E}}
$$

Setting (T) equal to Earth's rotational period gives the geostationary orbital radius:

$$
\boxed{r_{\rm geo}=\left(\frac{GM_ET^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}}
$$

The altitude above Earth's surface is

$$
\boxed{h_{\rm geo}=r_{\rm geo}-R_E}
$$

## Orbital Transfers

An **orbital transfer** is a controlled change from one orbit to another, usually achieved by changing the spacecraft's velocity through a propulsion maneuver. A brief change in velocity is called a **burn** or **impulse**.

Changing the velocity changes the spacecraft's orbital energy and angular momentum, thereby changing its orbit.

Increasing the speed of a spacecraft in a circular orbit generally places it onto an elliptical transfer orbit whose periapsis is near the original orbit. A subsequent burn can then circularize the spacecraft at the new altitude.

The most important simple transfer between two coplanar circular orbits is the **Hohmann transfer**.

### Hohmann Transfer

A **Hohmann transfer** is an ideal two-impulse transfer between two coplanar circular orbits.

Suppose a spacecraft moves from an inner circular orbit of radius $r_1$ to an outer circular orbit of radius $r_2$, where

$$
    r_2>r_1
$$

The transfer orbit is an ellipse with

$$
    \boxed{a_t = \frac{r_1 + r_2}{2}}
$$

#### First Burn

At the inner orbit, the spacecraft increases its velocity and enters the elliptical transfer orbit.

The circular velocity at $r_1$ is

$$
    v_{c1} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}
$$

Using the vis-viva equation, the velocity on the transfer orbit at $r_1$ is

$$
    \boxed{v_{t1} = \sqrt{GM \left( \frac{2}{r_1} - \frac{1}{a_t} \right)}}
$$

Therefore,

$$
    \boxed{\Delta v_1 = v_{t1} - v_{c1}}
$$

#### Second Burn

At the outer radius $r_2$, the spacecraft reaches apoapsis of the transfer ellipse.

Its transfer-orbit velocity is

$$
    \boxed{v_{t2} = \sqrt{GM \left( \frac{2}{r_2} - \frac{1}{a_t} \right)}}
$$

The circular velocity at $r_2$ is

$$
    v_{c2} = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}}
$$

The second burn circularizes the orbit:

$$
    \boxed{\Delta v_2 = v_{c2} - v_{t2}}
$$

Thus the total ideal velocity change is

$$
    \boxed{\Delta v_{\rm total} = \Delta v_1 + \Delta v_2}
$$

The Hohmann transfer is important because it provides an efficient two-impulse transfer between circular orbits under the ideal two-body approximation.

### Scattering Trajectories

An unbound particle approaching an attractive or repulsive center can be deflected and then escape to infinity, which is called scattering.

For a scattering trajectory, $r \to \infty$, both before and after the encounter.

For a particle arriving from infinity,

$$
\boxed{L=mv_\infty b}
$$

where $v_\infty$ is the speed at infinity and $b$ is the impact parameter, which describes how far the incoming trajectory would pass from the center if there were no interaction.

The angle between the incoming and outgoing asymptotic directions is related to the **scattering angle**.

### Orbital Precession

Orbital precession is the rotation of the orbital ellipse.

For an ideal inverse-square central force, the orbit is a closed conic section. However, more general central potentials do not necessarily produce closed orbits. If the angle between successive periapses differs from $2 \pi$, the orbit does not close after one revolution. The point of closest approach can gradually rotate from one revolution to the next.

A small deviation from the inverse-square potential can therefore produce a slowly rotating orbit.

Orbital precession occurs in several physical situations, including:

- non-ideal gravitational fields,
- perturbations from other bodies,
- oblateness of a rotating body,
- relativistic corrections to Newtonian gravity.
