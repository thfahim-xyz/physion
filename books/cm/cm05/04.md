# Orbital Mechanics

## Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

### Law of Orbits

The orbit of a planet is an ellipse with the Sun at one of the two foci.

### Law of Areas

A line segment joining a planet and the Sun sweeps out equal areas during equal intervals of time.

### Law of Periods

The square of the period of any planet is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit.

$$
    \boxed{T^2 = \frac{4 \pi^2}{GM} a^2}
$$

# Orbital Mechanics

The motion of a particle under a central force can be reduced to an equation for its orbit (r(\theta)). For the inverse-square gravitational force, this equation produces conic-section trajectories and leads to Kepler's laws and the standard results of orbital mechanics.



## Orbit Equation

The **orbit equation** describes the trajectory of a particle as

$$
\boxed{r=r(\theta)}
$$

rather than as a function of time.

For central-force motion,

$$
L=mr^2\dot\theta
$$

is conserved. Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\dot\theta=\frac{L}{mr^2}.
}
$$

The radial equation is

$$
m(\ddot r-r\dot\theta^2)=F(r).
$$

Using the angular-momentum relation to eliminate time derivatives leads to a differential equation involving only (r) and (\theta).

It is convenient to introduce

$$
\boxed{u=\frac1r}.
$$

The resulting orbit equation can be written in terms of (u(\theta)) using Binet's equation.



# Binet's Equation

Let

$$
u=\frac1r.
$$

Since

$$
L=mr^2\dot\theta,
$$

we have

$$
\dot\theta=\frac{Lu^2}{m}.
$$

Using

$$
r=\frac1u
$$

and differentiating with respect to time gives

$$
\dot r
=

-\frac{L}{m}\frac{du}{d\theta}.
$$

A second differentiation gives

$$
\ddot r
=

-\frac{L^2u^2}{m^2}
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}.
$$

Substituting into the radial equation yields **Binet's equation**:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}+u
=

-\frac{m}{L^2u^2}F\left(\frac1u\right).
}
$$

For a general central force, this equation determines the orbit (u(\theta)).

For an inverse-square attractive force,

$$
F(r)=-\frac{k}{r^2}=-ku^2,
$$

so Binet's equation becomes

$$
\boxed{
\frac{d^2u}{d\theta^2}+u
=

\frac{mk}{L^2}.
}
$$

Its solution is

$$
\boxed{
u=\frac{mk}{L^2}(1+e\cos\theta)
}
$$

after choosing the origin of (\theta) appropriately.

Since (u=1/r),

$$
\boxed{
r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta}
}
$$

where

$$
\boxed{
p=\frac{L^2}{mk}
}
$$

is the **semilatus rectum** and (e) is the eccentricity.

This is the equation of a conic section.



# Conic-Section Trajectories

The orbit

$$
\boxed{
r=\frac{p}{1+e\cos\theta}
}
$$

describes a conic section with the force center at one focus.

The type of orbit depends on the eccentricity (e):

| Eccentricity | Orbit     | Energy |
| ------ | --------- | ------ |
| (e=0)        | Circle    | (E<0)  |
| (0<e<1)      | Ellipse   | (E<0)  |
| (e=1)        | Parabola  | (E=0)  |
| (e>1)        | Hyperbola | (E>0)  |

Thus the sign of the orbital energy determines whether the trajectory is bound or unbound.



# Eccentricity

The **eccentricity** (e) measures the shape of a conic orbit.

For an inverse-square attractive force,

$$
\boxed{
e=
\sqrt{1+\frac{2EL^2}{mk^2}}
}
$$

where (E) is the orbital energy and (L) is the angular momentum.

Therefore:

$$
E<0\Rightarrow e<1,
$$

$$
E=0\Rightarrow e=1,
$$

$$
E>0\Rightarrow e>1.
$$

For a circular orbit,

$$
e=0.
$$

For gravitational motion,

$$
k=GMm
$$

and hence

$$
\boxed{
e=
\sqrt{1+\frac{2EL^2}{G^2M^2m^3}}.
}
$$



# Bound and Unbound Trajectories

The effective-potential equation is

$$
E=
\frac12m\dot r^2+
U_{\rm eff}(r).
$$

For an attractive inverse-square force,

$$
U(r)=-\frac{k}{r}.
$$

Thus,

$$
U_{\rm eff}(r)
=

-\frac{k}{r}
+
\frac{L^2}{2mr^2}.
$$

### Bound trajectories

If

$$
\boxed{E<0},
$$

the particle remains confined to a finite range of (r).

For the ideal inverse-square force, the orbit is an ellipse, with the circle as the special case (e=0).

### Unbound trajectories

If

$$
\boxed{E\ge0},
$$

the particle can escape to infinity.

The cases are:

$$
E=0\Rightarrow \text{parabolic trajectory},
$$

$$
E>0\Rightarrow \text{hyperbolic trajectory}.
$$



# Scattering Trajectories

An unbound particle approaching an attractive or repulsive center can be deflected and then escape to infinity.

This is called **scattering**.

For a scattering trajectory,

$$
r\rightarrow\infty
$$

both before and after the encounter.

The trajectory is therefore an unbound orbit, usually hyperbolic for an attractive inverse-square force.

The angle between the incoming and outgoing asymptotic directions is related to the **scattering angle**.

The impact parameter (b) describes how far the incoming trajectory would pass from the center if there were no interaction.

For a particle arriving from infinity,

$$
\boxed{
L=mv_\infty b
}
$$

where (v_\infty) is the speed at infinity.

Scattering therefore provides a direct connection between the geometry of an encounter and its angular momentum.



# Closest and Farthest Approach

At a radial turning point,

$$
\dot r=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
E=U_{\rm eff}(r).
$$

The smallest allowed radius is the **closest approach** or periapsis distance:

$$
\boxed{r_{\min}=r_p}.
$$

For a bound elliptical orbit, the largest radius is the **farthest approach** or apoapsis distance:

$$
\boxed{r_{\max}=r_a}.
$$

For an ellipse,

$$
\boxed{
r_p=\frac{p}{1+e}
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
r_a=\frac{p}{1-e}.
}
$$

Since

$$
p=a(1-e^2),
$$

these become

$$
\boxed{
r_p=a(1-e)
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
r_a=a(1+e).
}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
a=\frac{r_p+r_a}{2}
}
$$

where (a) is the semimajor axis.



# Orbital Precession

For an ideal inverse-square central force, the orbit is a closed conic section.

However, more general central potentials do not necessarily produce closed orbits.

The point of closest approach can gradually rotate from one revolution to the next. This rotation of the orbital ellipse is called **orbital precession**.

If the angle between successive periapses differs from (2\pi), the orbit does not close after one revolution.

A small deviation from the inverse-square potential can therefore produce a slowly rotating orbit.

Orbital precession occurs in several physical situations, including:

* non-ideal gravitational fields,
* perturbations from other bodies,
* oblateness of a rotating body,
* relativistic corrections to Newtonian gravity.

Thus a useful distinction is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{inverse-square force}
\rightarrow
\text{closed conic orbit}
}
$$

whereas

$$
\boxed{
\text{perturbed central force}
\rightarrow
\text{generally precessing orbit}.
}
$$



# Kepler's Laws of Planetary Motion

For gravitational motion, Newton's inverse-square law leads to the three empirical laws formulated by Johannes Kepler.

## First Law — Law of Orbits

Each planet moves in an ellipse with the Sun at one focus.

$$
\boxed{\text{Planetary orbit = ellipse}}
$$

The circle is a special case of an ellipse.



## Second Law — Law of Areas

The line joining the planet to the Sun sweeps out equal areas in equal times:

$$
\boxed{
\frac{dA}{dt}=\text{constant}.
}
$$

This follows directly from angular-momentum conservation.



## Third Law — Harmonic Law

For planets orbiting the same central mass,

$$
\boxed{
T^2\propto a^3
}
$$

where (T) is the orbital period and (a) is the semimajor axis.

More precisely,

$$
\boxed{
T^2=
\frac{4\pi^2}{GM}a^3
}
$$

when the central mass (M) dominates.

For the exact two-body problem,

$$
\boxed{
T^2=
\frac{4\pi^2}{G(m_1+m_2)}a^3.
}
$$



# Circular Orbits

A circular orbit has

$$
r=a=\text{constant}.
$$

For a gravitational force,

$$
\frac{GMm}{r^2}
=

\frac{mv^2}{r}.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}.
}
$$

The orbital angular velocity is

$$
\omega=\frac vr
$$

so

$$
\boxed{
\omega=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r^3}}.
}
$$

The angular momentum is

$$
\boxed{
L=m\sqrt{GMr}.
}
$$



# Elliptical Orbits

For an elliptical orbit,

$$
\boxed{
r=\frac{a(1-e^2)}{1+e\cos\theta}.
}
$$

Here:

* (a) is the semimajor axis,
* (e) is the eccentricity,
* (a(1-e^2)) is the semilatus rectum.

The periapsis and apoapsis distances are

$$
\boxed{
r_p=a(1-e)
}
$$

and

$$
\boxed{
r_a=a(1+e).
}
$$

The orbital energy depends only on the semimajor axis:

$$
\boxed{
E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}.
}
$$

This is one of the most important results for bound gravitational orbits.



# Orbital Velocity

For a circular orbit,

$$
\boxed{
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}.
}
$$

For an elliptical orbit, the speed changes continuously along the orbit.

The planet moves fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis.

The general velocity is given by the vis-viva equation:

$$
\boxed{
v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
}
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
v=\sqrt{GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right)}.
}
$$

At periapsis,

$$
\boxed{
v_p=
\sqrt{
\frac{GM}{a}
\frac{1+e}{1-e}
}.
}
$$

At apoapsis,

$$
\boxed{
v_a=
\sqrt{
\frac{GM}{a}
\frac{1-e}{1+e}
}.
}
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{v_p>v_a}.
$$



# Orbital Period

The orbital period (T) is the time required for one complete orbit.

For a circular orbit,

$$
T=\frac{2\pi r}{v}.
$$

Using

$$
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}},
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM}}.
}
$$

For an elliptical orbit,

$$
\boxed{
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}}.
}
$$

Therefore the period depends on the **semimajor axis**, not directly on eccentricity.

This gives Kepler's third law:

$$
\boxed{
T^2\propto a^3.
}
$$



# Orbital Energy

For a gravitational orbit,

$$
U=-\frac{GMm}{r}.
$$

The total orbital energy is

$$
E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r}.
$$

For a bound elliptical orbit,

$$
\boxed{
E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}.
}
$$

Thus:

$$
\boxed{
E<0\quad\text{for bound orbits}.
}
$$

For a parabolic escape orbit,

$$
\boxed{E=0}.
$$

For a hyperbolic orbit,

$$
\boxed{E>0}.
$$

The more negative the energy, the more tightly bound the orbit.



# Vis-Viva Equation

Combining

$$
E=-\frac{GMm}{2a}
$$

with

$$
E=\frac12mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r},
$$

we obtain

$$
\boxed{
v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
}
$$

This is the **vis-viva equation**.

It determines the orbital speed at any distance (r) for an orbit with semimajor axis (a).

For a circular orbit,

$$
r=a,
$$

so

$$
v^2=\frac{GM}{r},
$$

recovering the circular-orbit velocity.



# Escape Orbits

An object escapes the gravitational influence of a central mass if its total orbital energy is at least zero.

At the limiting case,

$$
E=0.
$$

For an object launched from radius (r),

$$
\frac12mv_{\rm esc}^2-\frac{GMm}{r}=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
v_{\rm esc}
=

\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}}.
}
$$

The escape velocity is therefore

$$
\boxed{
v_{\rm esc}=\sqrt2,v_{\rm circular}.
}
$$

An object launched exactly at escape velocity follows a parabolic trajectory.

If

$$
v>v_{\rm esc},
$$

the trajectory is hyperbolic.

If

$$
v<v_{\rm esc},
$$

the object remains gravitationally bound, assuming no other forces act.



# Geostationary Orbits

A **geostationary satellite** is a satellite in a circular orbit that appears stationary above one point on Earth's equator.

Three conditions are required:

1. The orbit must be circular.
2. The orbit must lie in Earth's equatorial plane.
3. The satellite must orbit in the same direction as Earth's rotation and have the same angular period as Earth's rotation.

For a circular orbit,

$$
T=2\pi\sqrt{\frac{r^3}{GM_E}}.
$$

Setting (T) equal to Earth's rotational period gives the geostationary orbital radius:

$$
\boxed{
r_{\rm geo}
=

\left(
\frac{GM_ET^2}{4\pi^2}
\right)^{1/3}.
}
$$

The altitude above Earth's surface is

$$
\boxed{
h_{\rm geo}=r_{\rm geo}-R_E.
}
$$



# Satellite Motion

A satellite in orbit is continuously falling toward the central body while its tangential velocity causes it to continually miss the surface.

For a circular orbit,

$$
\boxed{
v=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r}}.
}
$$

As the orbital radius increases:

$$
v\propto r^{-1/2},
$$

so higher circular orbits have lower orbital speeds.

However, the orbital period increases:

$$
T\propto r^{3/2}.
$$

For an elliptical orbit, the satellite's speed varies according to

$$
\boxed{
v^2=GM\left(\frac2r-\frac1a\right).
}
$$

It moves fastest at periapsis and slowest at apoapsis.



# Orbital Transfers

An **orbital transfer** is a controlled change from one orbit to another, usually achieved by changing the spacecraft's velocity through a propulsion maneuver.

A brief change in velocity is called a **burn** or **impulse**.

Changing the velocity changes the spacecraft's orbital energy and angular momentum, thereby changing its orbit.

For example, increasing the speed of a spacecraft in a circular orbit generally places it onto an elliptical transfer orbit whose periapsis is near the original orbit.

A subsequent burn can then circularize the spacecraft at the new altitude.

The most important simple transfer between two coplanar circular orbits is the **Hohmann transfer**.



# Hohmann Transfer

A **Hohmann transfer** is an ideal two-impulse transfer between two coplanar circular orbits.

Suppose a spacecraft moves from an inner circular orbit of radius (r_1) to an outer circular orbit of radius (r_2), where

$$
r_2>r_1.
$$

The transfer orbit is an ellipse with

$$
\boxed{
a_t=\frac{r_1+r_2}{2}.
}
$$

### First Burn

At the inner orbit, the spacecraft increases its velocity and enters the elliptical transfer orbit.

The circular velocity at (r_1) is

$$
v_{c1}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_1}}.
$$

Using the vis-viva equation, the velocity on the transfer orbit at (r_1) is

$$
\boxed{
v_{t1}
=

\sqrt{
GM\left(
\frac2{r_1}-\frac1{a_t}
\right)
}.
}
$$

Therefore,

$$
\boxed{
\Delta v_1=v_{t1}-v_{c1}.
}
$$



### Second Burn

At the outer radius (r_2), the spacecraft reaches apoapsis of the transfer ellipse.

Its transfer-orbit velocity is

$$
\boxed{
v_{t2}
=

\sqrt{
GM\left(
\frac2{r_2}-\frac1{a_t}
\right)
}.
}
$$

The circular velocity at (r_2) is

$$
v_{c2}=\sqrt{\frac{GM}{r_2}}.
$$

The second burn circularizes the orbit:

$$
\boxed{
\Delta v_2=v_{c2}-v_{t2}.
}
$$

Thus the total ideal velocity change is

$$
\boxed{
\Delta v_{\rm total}
=

\Delta v_1+\Delta v_2.
}
$$

The Hohmann transfer is important because it provides an efficient two-impulse transfer between circular orbits under the ideal two-body approximation.



# Orbital Mechanics: Core Structure

The main logical chain of gravitational orbital mechanics is:

$$
\boxed{
\text{Inverse-square force}
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Binet's equation}
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Conic-section orbit}
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{
e,\quad E,\quad L
}
$$

$$
\Downarrow
$$

$$
\boxed{
\text{Bound / unbound motion}
}
$$

For bound motion:

$$
\boxed{
\text{ellipse}
\rightarrow
a,e
\rightarrow
T,;v,;E
}
$$

while for unbound motion:

$$
\boxed{
E\ge0
\rightarrow
\text{parabolic/hyperbolic trajectories}
\rightarrow
\text{escape and scattering}.
}
$$

Finally, these results form the foundation of practical orbital mechanics:

$$
\boxed{
\text{circular orbits}
\rightarrow
\text{satellites}
\rightarrow
\text{geostationary orbits}
\rightarrow
\text{orbital transfers}
\rightarrow
\text{Hohmann transfer}.
}
$$
