# Two-Body Problem

## Two-Body System

The **two-body problem** concerns the motion of two particles interacting through a mutual force. The problem can be separated into two independent parts:

1. the motion of the center of mass of the system
2. the motion of one body relative to the other

For a central interaction or central force, the second part becomes an ordinary **one-body central-force problem**.

Consider two particles with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, positions $\mathbf r_1$ and $\mathbf r_2$, and they interact through an internal force $\mathbf F$.

By Newton's third law,

$$
\mathbf F_{12}=-\mathbf F_{21}.
$$

The equations of motion are therefore

$$
m_1\ddot{\mathbf r}_1=\mathbf F_{21},
\quad
m_2\ddot{\mathbf r}_2=\mathbf F_{12}.
$$

For a central interaction, the force depends only on the separation and acts along the line joining the particles.

$$
r=|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|
$$

Thus,

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf F_{12}
=
F(r)\hat{\mathbf r}
}
$$

where the relative position vector is

$$
\boxed{
\mathbf r=\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2.
}
$$

The two equations above are coupled because each particle's motion depends on the other. The purpose of the two-body reduction is to uncouple them.

### Center-of-Mass and Relative Coordinates

The coupled equations of motion for a two-body system can be uncoupled using the center-of-mass and relative position as coordinates of the system, instead of the original positions of the particles.

The corresponding transformation $(\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2) \to (\mathbf R, \mathbf r)$, where $\mathbf R$ is the center-of-mass and $\mathbf r$ is the relative position, is:

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf R = \frac{m_1 \mathbf r_1 + m_2 \mathbf r_2}{M}},
    \quad
    \boxed{\mathbf r = \mathbf r_1 - \mathbf r_2}
$$

where $M = m_1 + m_2$ is the total mass. $\mathbf R$ describes the position of the entire system's center of mass and $\mathbf r$ describes the position of particle $1$ relative to particle $2$.

The inverse transformation $(\mathbf R, \mathbf r) \to (\mathbf r_1, \mathbf r_2)$ returns the original coordinates and is given by

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf r_1 = \mathbf R + \frac{m_2}{M} \mathbf r},
    \quad
    \boxed{\mathbf r_2 = \mathbf R - \frac{m_1}{M} \mathbf r}
$$

### Equation of Motion for Center-of-Mass Coordinate

The two-body system, when isolated, moves according to

$$
    \boxed{\ddot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf 0} \implies \mathbf R = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} \, t
$$

where $\mathbf V_{\text{CM}} = \dot{\mathbf R}$ is the velocity of the center of mass, which is constant for the isolated system. This completely separates the translational motion of the system from its internal motion.

When an external force $\mathbf F_{\textrm{ext}}$ is applied to the two-body system, the center-of-mass moves according to

$$
    \mathbf F_{\textrm{ext}} = M \ddot{\mathbf R}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The center-of-mass of the two-body system is

$$
    \mathbf R = \frac{m_1 \mathbf r_1 + m_2 \mathbf r_2}{M}
$$

where $M = m_1 + m_2$ is the total mass.

The net internal force is, therefore,

$$
    \mathbf F = M \ddot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf F_{21} + \mathbf F_{12}
$$

According to Newton's third law, the internal forces cancel $(\mathbf F_{21}+\mathbf F_{12}=0)$. So, $\mathbf F = 0$. Thus, if there is no external force,

$$
    \mathbf F_{\textrm{net}} = \mathbf F = 0
$$

$$
    \implies M \ddot{\mathbf R} = 0
$$

$$
    \implies \ddot{\mathbf R} = 0
$$

Which, when solved, gives

$$
    \mathbf R = \mathbf R_0 + \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} \, t, \quad \mathbf V_{\text{CM}} = \text{constant}
$$

Hence the center of mass moves with uniform velocity.

</div>

### Equation of Motion for Relative Coordinate

The equation of motion for the relative coordinate is

$$
    \boxed{\mu \ddot{\mathbf r} = \mathbf F}
$$

where $\mathbf F$ is the internal force (i.e., the central force) and $\mu$ is the reduced mass, defined as

$$
    \boxed{\mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2}}
$$

The equation of motion has exactly the same mathematical form as Newton's second law for a single particle. Therefore, the original two-body problem is equivalent to: A single fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ moving in the interaction potential as a function of the relative coordinate $\mathbf r$.

If one mass is much heavier than the other $(m_2 \gg m_1)$. Then

$$
    \mu \approx m_1, \quad \mathbf R \approx \mathbf r_2
$$

Thus the center of mass lies very close to the massive body, and the two-body problem becomes approximately the familiar problem of a small body moving around a fixed massive body. This is why the motion of a planet around the Sun can often be treated as the motion of a particle of mass in the Sun's gravitational field.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The equations of motion for the individual particles are

$$
    m_1 \ddot{\mathbf r}_1 = \mathbf F, \quad m_2 \ddot{\mathbf r}_2 = -\mathbf F
$$

$$
    \implies \ddot{\mathbf r}_1 = \frac{\mathbf F}{m_1}, \quad \ddot{\mathbf r}_2 = - \frac{\mathbf F}{m_2}
$$

Subtracting the equations give

$$
    \ddot{\mathbf r}_1 - \ddot{\mathbf r}_2 = \mathbf F \left( \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2} \right)
$$

Define the reduced mass $\mu$ as

$$
    \mu = \frac{m_1 m_2}{m_1 + m_2} \implies \frac{1}{\mu} = \frac{1}{m_1} + \frac{1}{m_2}
$$

And since

$$
    \ddot{\mathbf r}_1 - \ddot{\mathbf r}_2 = \ddot{\mathbf r}
$$

The relative equation becomes

$$
    \ddot{\mathbf r} = \frac{\mathbf F}{\mu} \implies \boxed{\mu\ddot{\mathbf r}=\mathbf F}
$$

</div>

## Energy of Two-Body Systems

The total energy of a two-body system is

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} M \lvert \dot{\mathbf R} \rvert^2 + \left[ \frac{1}{2} \mu \lvert \mathbf r \rvert^2 + U(r) \right]}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The total kinetic energy of the two particles in a two-body system is

$$
    K = \frac{1}{2} m_1 v_1^2 + \frac{1}{2} m_2 v_2^2
$$

Using the center-of-mass and relative coordinates, this separates into

$$
    K = \frac{1}{2} M \lvert \dot{\mathbf R} \rvert^2 + \frac{1}{2} \mu \lvert \mathbf r \rvert^2 = \frac{1}{2} M V_{\textrm{CM}}^2 + \frac{1}{2} \mu v^2
$$

Therefore, for a conservative interaction, the total energy is

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} M \lvert \dot{\mathbf R} \rvert^2 + \left[ \frac{1}{2} \mu \lvert \dot{\mathbf r} \rvert^2 + U(r) \right]} = E_{\textrm{CM}} + E_{\textrm{rel}}
$$

The center-of-mass motion and internal relative motion can therefore be treated independently.

</div>

## Energy of the Relative Motion

The orbit of a two-body system depends on the relative motion of the system. The energy of the relative motion of particles in a two-body system under a central force is

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} \mu \dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2 \mu r^2} + U(r)} = \frac{1}{2} \mu \dot r^2 + U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)
$$

where $U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$ is the effective potential, given by

$$
    U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = \frac{L^2}{2 \mu r^2} + U(r)
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The relative energy of the system is

$$
    E_{\textrm{rel}} = \frac{1}{2} \mu \lvert \dot{\mathbf r} \rvert^2 + U(r)
$$

For a central interaction, the relative coordinate behaves exactly like the position of a particle moving under a central force. So, in polar coordinates,

$$
    \lvert \dot{\mathbf r} \rvert^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2 \dot \theta^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
    E_{\textrm{rel}} = \frac{1}{2} \mu \dot r^2 + \frac{1}{2} \mu r^2 \dot \theta^2 + U(r)
$$

For a central interaction, the angular momentum is conserved.

$$
    L = \mu r^2 \dot \theta \implies \frac{1}{2} \mu r^2 \dot \theta^2 = \frac{L^2}{2 \mu r^2}
$$

Thus, the energy equation for relative motion is

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} \mu \dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2 \mu r^2} + U(r)}
$$

Thus both particles orbit their common center of mass while their separation vector follows the relative orbit.

</div>
