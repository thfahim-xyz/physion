# Central Force Dynamics

## Central Forces

A **central force** is a force whose magnitude depends only on the distance from a fixed point and whose direction is along the line joining the particle to that point:

$$
    \boxed{\mathbf F (\mathbf r) = F(r) \hat{\mathbf r}}
$$

where $r = \lvert \mathbf r \rvert$ and $\hat{\mathbf r}$ is the radial unit vector. The fixed point is called the **center of force**.

The sign of $F(r)$ determines whether the force is attractive or repulsive. $F(r)<0$ means the force is attractive; $F(r)>0$ means the force is repulsive.

Examples:

- Gravitational Force:
    
    $$
        \mathbf F = - \frac{GMm}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf r}
    $$

- Electrostatic Force:

    $$
        \mathbf F = \frac{k q_1 q_2}{r^2} \hat{\mathbf r}
    $$

Central forces possess spherical symmetry about the center of force. Rotating the entire system around the center does not change the force.

### Conservative Central Forces

A central force is conservative if it can be derived from a potential energy $U(r)$:

$$
    \boxed{F(r) = -\frac{\mathrm dU}{\mathrm dr}}
$$

The work done by the force between two positions depends only on the initial and final distances:

$$
    W = U(r_1) - U(r_0).
$$

Therefore, the mechanical energy $E = K + U$ is conserved.

Both gravitational and electrostatic forces are conservative central forces.

### Inverse-Power-Law Forces

Inverse-power-law forces are central forces that are inversely proportional to a power of distance from the center.

$$
    F(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^n}
$$

For an attractive force,

$$
    F(r) = - \frac{k}{r^n} \implies U(r) = - \frac{k}{n-1} \frac{1}{r^{n-1}}
$$

when the potential is chosen to vanish at infinity.

#### Inverse-Square Forces

An inverse-square force has the form

$$
    \boxed{F(r) \propto \frac{1}{r^2}}.
$$

For an attractive inverse-square force,

$$
    F(r) = - \frac{k}{r^2} \implies \boxed{U(r)=-\frac{k}{r}}
$$

The gravitational and electrostatic forces are inverse-square forces. The trajectories of inverse-square central forces are conic sections (e.g. circles, ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas).

## Angular Momentum Conservation

### Conservation of Angular Momentum

For a central force, the torque about the center of force is zero. So both the magnitude and direction of angular momentum are conserved.

$$
    \boxed{\frac{\mathrm d \mathbf L}{\mathrm dt} = 0} \implies \mathbf L = \text{constant}
$$

In polar coordinates, $L = m r^2 \dot \theta$. Thus

$$
    \boxed{r^2 \dot \theta = \text{constant}}
$$

### Planarity of Motion

Since $\mathbf L = \mathbf r \times \mathbf p$ is constant in both magnitude and direction, $\mathbf r$ must always lie in the plane perpendicular to $\mathbf L$. Hence
motion under a central force is confined to a plane. This is called the planarity of motion.

This reduces what appears to be a three-dimensional problem to a two-dimensional one. Therefore the choice of plane of motion as the $xy$-plane and the usage of polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ is convenient.

## Areal Velocity

Areal velocity is given by

$$
    \boxed{A_v = \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dt} = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot \theta = \frac{L}{2m}}
$$

which is constant under central forces because $L$ is conserved.

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dt} = \text{constant}
$$

The particle sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**. This is the origin of **Kepler's second law** for gravitational motion.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider the small area swept out by the position vector during a time $\mathrm dt$.

$$
    \mathrm dA = \frac{1}{2} r^2 \, \mathrm d \theta.
$$

Therefore,

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dt}= \frac{1}{2} r^2 \dot \theta
$$

Using $L = m r^2 \dot \theta$, we obtain

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dA}{\mathrm dt}= \frac{L}{2m}
$$

</div>

## Equation of Motion Under Central Forces

The equation of motion under the central force $F(r)$ is

$$
    \boxed{m \ddot r = F(r) + m r \dot \theta^2}
$$

which is the radial equation for a particle moving under the influence of a central force.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

For planar motion, the acceleration in polar coordinates is

$$
    \mathbf a = \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r} + \left( 2 \dot r \dot \theta + r \ddot \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}
$$

Newton's second law gives

$$
    \mathbf F = m \mathbf a
$$

Therefore, for a general force,

$$
\begin{aligned}
    \mathbf F &= m \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r} + m \left( 2 \dot r \dot \theta + r \ddot \theta \right) \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} \\
            &= F_r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + F_{\theta} \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta}
\end{aligned}
$$

where

- Radial force: $F_r = m (\ddot r - r \dot \theta^2)$
- Transverse force: $F_{\theta} = m (r \ddot \theta + 2 \dot r \dot \theta)$

For a central force, $F_\theta = 0$. So only the radial component of force remains.

$$
    \mathbf F = m \left( \ddot r - r \dot{\theta}^2 \right) \, \hat{\mathbf r}
$$

**Radial Equation of Motion:** For a central force, $\mathbf F = F(r) \hat{\mathbf r}$. Thus the radial equation is

$$
    F(r) = m \left( \ddot r - r \dot \theta^2 \right) \implies \boxed{m \ddot r = F(r) + m r \dot \theta^2}
$$

The term $m r \dot \theta^2$ appears because the radial component of acceleration contains the term $- r \dot \theta^2$. It is important not to interpret this as an additional real force in an inertial frame.

**Transverse Equation of Motion:** For a central force, $F_{\theta} = 0$. Thus the transverse equation is

$$
    m (r \ddot \theta + 2 \dot r \dot \theta) = 0 \implies m r \ddot \theta + 2 m \dot r \dot \theta = 0
$$

But

$$
    m r \ddot \theta + 2 m \dot r \dot \theta = \frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt} (m r^2 \dot \theta) = \frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm dt}
$$

Hence,

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dL}{\mathrm dt} = 0 \implies L = \text{constant}
$$

So the transverse equation is simply another expression of angular-momentum conservation.

</div>

## Energy Under Central Forces

Energy equation for a particle moving under a central force is

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2 m r^2} + U(r)}
$$

This equation allows the two-dimensional orbital problem to be treated as an effective one-dimensional radial problem.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

In polar coordinates, the speed of a particle is given by

$$
    v^2 = \dot r^2 + r^2 \dot \theta^2
$$

The kinetic energy is

$$
    K = \frac{1}{2} m v^2
$$

Thus, we have

$$
    K = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + \frac{1}{2} m r^2 \dot \theta^2
$$

For a conservative central force,

$$
    E = K + U(r)
$$

Therefore,

$$
    E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + \frac{1}{2} m r^2 \dot \theta^2 + U(r)
$$

Using $L = m r^2 \dot \theta$, the angular kinetic-energy term becomes

$$
    \frac{1}{2} m r^2 \dot \theta^2 = \frac{L^2}{2 m r^2}
$$

Thus,

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + \frac{L^2}{2 m r^2} + U(r)}
$$

</div>

## Effective Potential

The effective potential can be defined as

$$
    \boxed{U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r) = U(r) + \frac{L^2}{2mr^2}}
$$

In terms of effective potential, the energy equation becomes

$$
    \boxed{E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)}
$$

The first term represents radial kinetic energy, while $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$ contains both the actual potential energy and the contribution associated with angular motion. The term $\dfrac{L^2}{2mr^2}$ is sometimes called the **centrifugal potential**. It grows rapidly as $r\to0$ whenever $L\neq0$.

### Reduction to One-Dimensional Radial Motion

From rearranging the energy equation, we get

$$
    \boxed{\dot r = \pm \sqrt{\frac{2}{m} \left[ E - U_{\mathrm{eff}} (r) \right]}}
$$

The radial motion is therefore mathematically equivalent to a one-dimensional particle moving in the potential $U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$. The original two-dimensional orbital problem has been separated into:

- angular motion determined by $L = m r^2 \dot \theta$
- radial motion determined by $E = \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 + U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$

**Radial Motion:** According to the energy equation,

$$
    \frac{1}{2} m \dot r^2 = E - U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)
$$

radial motion is possible only where $E \ge U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$. If $E<U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)$ the expression for $\dot r^2$ would be negative, so that region is physically inaccessible. The radial coordinate therefore oscillates between allowed values in a bound orbit.

## Turning Points

A turning point is a point where the radial velocity changes direction. At a turning point, $\dot r = 0$, therefore,

$$
    E = U_{\mathrm{eff}}(r)
$$

The two common types are:

- **Periapsis:** minimum radial distance $r = r_{\min}$

- **Apoapsis:** maximum radial distance $r = r_{\max}$

At these points the particle's velocity is purely transverse.

For a bound orbit, $r_{\min} \leq r \leq r_{\max}$

## Circular Orbits

A circular orbit is an orbit that has constant radius $r = r_0$. So, from the radial equation,

$$
    F(r_0) = - m r_0 \dot \theta^2 = - \frac{mv^2}{r_0}
$$

Thus the required central force must provide the inward centripetal acceleration.

In terms of the effective potential, a circular orbit occurs at an equilibrium point:

$$
    \boxed{\left. \frac{\mathrm dU_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr} \right|_{r=r_0} = 0}
$$

### Stability of Circular Orbits

A circular orbit can be stable or unstable depending on the shape of the effective potential near $r_0$.

- **Stable Circular Orbit:** In a stable orbit, A small radial displacement increases the effective potential and produces a restoring tendency. In stable orbits, $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has a local minimum.

    $$
        \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_0} > 0
    $$

- **Unstable Circular Orbit:** In an unstable orbit, A small displacement moves the particle away from the circular orbit. In unstable orbits, $U_{\mathrm{eff}}$ has a local maximum.

    $$
        \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_0} < 0
    $$

If, for a circular orbit,

$$
    \left. \frac{\mathrm d^2 U_{\mathrm{eff}}}{\mathrm dr^2} \right|_{r=r_0} = 0
$$

then the second-derivative test is inconclusive and higher-order terms must be examined.
