# Section 1

## Assumptions of Kinetic Theory of Gases

Kinetic theory of gases explains the macroscopic behaviour of gases using the microscopic motions of gas particles based on the following simplifying assumptions:

- The gas consists of small particles. The smallness is such that the sum of the volumes of the individual gas particles is negligible compared to the volume of the gas container.
- The number of particles is so large that a statistical treatment of the problem is well justified.
- The rapidly moving particles constantly collide among themselves and with the walls of the container, with the collisions being perfectly elastic.
- Except during collisions, the interaction among particles are negligible. They exert no other forces on one another. Furthermore, the interactions are strictly binary and uncorrelated.

## Ideal Gas

An ideal gas is a theoretical gas composed of randomly moving point particles that are not subject to interparticle interactions. This gas is described by the ideal gas law and is usually assumed to be monatomic.

## Maxwell–Boltzmann Distribution

Velocity distribution function:

$$
    p(v) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 \exp \left( -\frac{mv^2}{2k_B T} \right)
$$

Kinetic energy distribution function:

$$
    p_K(K) = 2 \, \sqrt{\frac{K}{\pi}} \left( \frac{1}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2} \exp \left( -\frac{K}{k_B T} \right)
$$

where $m$, $v$ and $K$ is the mass, speed and kinetic energy of each gas particle.

## Most Probable Speed

Most probable speed is the speed of most of the particles in an ideal gas. It is the speed for which the value of the Maxwell-Boltzmann distribution function is maximum.

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dv} = 0 \implies \boxed{v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}}}
$$

where $M$ is the molar mass of the gas.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Most probable speed is found by maximizing the distribution function.

$$
    p(v) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 \exp \left( -\frac{mv^2}{2k_B T} \right)
$$

Let

$$
    C = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2}, \quad a = \frac{m}{2k_B T}
$$

so that

$$
    p(v) = C v^2 \exp \left( -a v^2 \right)
$$

For most probable speed,

$$
    \frac{\mathrm dp}{\mathrm dv} = 0
$$

$$
    \implies 2v e^{-a v^2} (1 - a v^2) = 0
$$

$$
    \implies v = \frac{1}{\sqrt{a}} \implies \boxed{v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2k_B T}{m}}}
$$

Since $k_B = R/N_A$, we can also write the above equation as

$$
    v_p = \sqrt{\frac{2RT}{M}}
$$

</div>

## Mean Speed

$$
    v_m = \langle v \rangle = \int_0^{\infty} v \, p(v) \, \mathrm d v
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{v_m = \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}} = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}}} = \frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}} v_p
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The mean speed is defined as

$$
    v_m = \langle v \rangle = \int_0^{\infty} v \, p(v) \, \mathrm d v
$$

The velocity distribution function is

$$
    p(v) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 \exp \left( -\frac{mv^2}{2k_B T} \right)
$$

Let

$$
    C = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2}, \quad a = \frac{m}{2k_B T}
$$

so that

$$
    p(v) = C v^2 \exp \left( -a v^2 \right)
$$

Thus

$$
    v_m = \langle v \rangle = C \int_0^{\infty} v^3 e^{-a v^2} \, \mathrm d v
$$

This integral is the following Gaussian integral:

$$
    \int_0^{\infty} x^3 e^{-a x^2} \, \mathrm d x = \frac{1}{2 a^2}
$$

Therefore

$$
    v_m = \langle v \rangle = \frac{C}{2 a^2}
$$

Substituting the constants $C$ and $a$ back, we get

$$
    \boxed{v_m = \sqrt{\frac{8k_B T}{\pi m}}}
$$

Since $k_B = R/N_A$, we can also write the above equation as

$$
    v_m = \sqrt{\frac{8RT}{\pi M}}
$$

</div>

## Mean Square Speed

$$
    v_{\textrm{ms}} = \langle v^2 \rangle = \int_0^{\infty} v^2 p(v) \, \mathrm d v
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{v_{\textrm{ms}} = \frac{3k_B T}{m} = \frac{3RT}{M}} = \frac{3}{2} v_p^2
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

The mean speed is defined as

$$
    v_{\textrm{ms}} = \langle v^2 \rangle = \int_0^{\infty} v^2 p(v) \, \mathrm d v
$$

The velocity distribution function is

$$
    p(v) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2} v^2 \exp \left( -\frac{mv^2}{2k_B T} \right)
$$

Let

$$
    C = \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \left( \frac{m}{k_B T} \right)^{3/2}, \quad a = \frac{m}{2k_B T}
$$

so that

$$
    p(v) = C v^2 \exp \left( -a v^2 \right)
$$

Thus

$$
    v_{\textrm{ms}} = \langle v^2 \rangle = C \int_0^{\infty} v^4 e^{-a v^2} \, \mathrm d v
$$

This integral is the following Gaussian integral:

$$
    \int_0^{\infty} x^4 e^{-a x^2} \, \mathrm d x = \frac{3 \sqrt{\pi}}{8 a^{5/2}}
$$

Therefore

$$
    v_{\textrm{ms}} = \langle v^2 \rangle = C \cdot \left( \frac{3 \sqrt{\pi}}{8 a^{5/2}} \right)
$$

Substituting the constants $C$ and $a$ back, we get

$$
    \boxed{v_{\textrm{ms}} = \frac{3k_B T}{m}}
$$

Since $k_B = R/N_A$, we can also write the above equation as

$$
    v_{\textrm{ms}} = \frac{3RT}{M}
$$

</div>

## Root Mean Square Speed

Root mean square speed is the square root of the mean square speed.

$$
    v_{rms} = \sqrt{v_{ms}}
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{v_{rms} = \sqrt{\frac{3k_B T}{m}} = \sqrt{\frac{3RT}{M}}} = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}} v_p
$$

## Pressure and Temperature in Microscopic Form

### Pressure

The microscopic interpretation of pressure is

$$
    \boxed{P = \frac{Nm}{3V} \langle v^2 \rangle} \implies P = \frac{1}{3} \rho \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

where

- $m$ is the mass of each molecule of the gas
- $N$ is the total number of molecules in the gass
- $v$ is the RMS speed of the gas molecules
- $\langle v^2 \rangle$ is the mean square speed
- $\rho$ is the mass density of the gas

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider an ideal gas containing $N$ molecules in a cubic container of volume $V=L^3$. Take one molecule of mass $m$, with velocity

$$
    \mathbf v = (v_x, v_y, v_z)
$$

We examine its collisions with the wall perpendicular to the $x$-axis. Before collision, the molecule has the momentum $p_x = m v_x$. After an elastic collision with the wall, the $x$-component reverses: $p_x' = -m v_x$. Therefore, the change in the molecule's momentum is

$$
    \Delta p_x = p_x' - p_x = -2 m v_x
$$

The wall receives the opposite momentum $(\Delta p_{\textrm{wall}} = 2 m v_x)$

The molecule travels from one $x$-wall to the opposite wall and back, a distance $2L$. Thus the time between collisions with the **same wall** is

$$
    \Delta t = \frac{2L}{v_x}.
$$

Therefore, the average force exerted by this molecule on the wall is

$$
    F_x = \frac{\Delta p_{\textrm{wall}}}{\Delta t} = \frac{2m v_x}{2L/v_x} \implies F_x = \frac{m v_x^2}{L}
$$

Now, for each molecule,

$$
    F_{ix} = \frac{m v_{ix}^2}{L}
$$

Therefore,

$$
    F_x = \frac{m}{L} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} v_{ix}^2
$$

Pressure is defined as force per area. The wall has area $A = L^2$. Thus

$$
    P = \frac{F_x}{A} = \frac{m}{L^3} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} v_{ix}^2
$$

Since $L^3 = V$,

$$
    P = \frac{m}{V} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} v_{ix}^2
$$

Mean-square velocity for $x$-component is

$$
    \langle v_x^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} v_{ix}^2
$$

Therefore, 

$$
    P = \frac{N m}{V} \langle v_x^2 \rangle
$$

Now, for an ideal gas in equilibrium, there is no preferred direction. The molecular motion is isotropic, so

$$
    \langle v_x^2\rangle = \langle v_y^2\rangle = \langle v_z^2\rangle
$$

And since the speed of the molecules (RMS speed) is given by

$$
    v^2 = v_x^2 + v_y^2 + v_z^2,
$$

taking averages gives

$$
    \langle v^2\rangle = 3 \langle v_x^2 \rangle
$$

Therefore, the pressure equation becomes

$$
    \boxed{P = \frac{Nm}{3V} \langle v^2 \rangle}
$$

This is the microscopic equation of pressure.

</div>

### Temperature

The microscopic interpretation of temperature is

$$
    \boxed{T = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m}{k_B} \langle v^2 \rangle}
$$

The temperature of an ideal gas is proportional to the mean translational kinetic energy of its molecules.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

An ideal gas obeys the ideal gas law

$$
    PV = N k_B T
$$

The microscopic form of pressure is

$$
    P = \frac{Nm}{3V} \langle v^2 \rangle \implies PV = \frac{1}{3} Nm \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

Therefore, 

$$
    N k_B T = \frac{1}{3} Nm \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

which gives

$$
    \boxed{T = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m}{k_B} \langle v^2 \rangle}
$$

This is the microscopic equation of temperature.

</div>

## Energy of Ideal Gases

### Kinetic Energy of an Ideal Gas

The translational kinetic energy of an ideal gas is

$$
    \boxed{K_{\textrm{trans}} = \frac{3}{2} N k_B T = \frac{3}{2} n R T}
$$

where

- $N$ is the total number of molecules of the gas
- $n$ is the number of moles of the gas
- $T$ is the temperature of the gas

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Consider $N$ molecules of an ideal gas, each of mass $m$. For the $i$-th molecule, its translational kinetic energy is

$$
    K_i = \frac{1}{2} m v_i^2
$$

The total kinetic energy is therefore

$$
    K = \sum_{i = 1}^N K_i = \frac{1}{2} m \sum_{i = 1}^N v_i^2
$$

Using the definition of the mean square speed,

$$
    \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i = 1}^{N} v_{i}^2
$$

Therefore,

$$
    K = \frac{1}{2} N m \langle v^2\rangle
$$

Now we need to express $\langle v^2\rangle$ in terms of temperature. From the microscopic form of temperature,

$$
    T = \frac{1}{3} \frac{m}{k_B} \langle v^2 \rangle \implies k_B T = \frac{1}{3} m \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

For $N$ molecules,

$$
    N k_B T = \frac{1}{3} N m \langle v^2 \rangle
$$

$$
    \implies N k_B T = \frac{2}{3} \frac{1}{2} N m \langle v^2 \rangle = \frac{2}{3} K
$$

$$
    \implies \boxed{K = \frac{3}{2} N k_B T}
$$

Since $N = n N_A$ and $R = N_A k_B$, we can also write the above equation as

$$
    K = \frac{3}{2} n R T
$$

</div>

## Internal Energy of an Ideal Gas

The internal energy of an ideal gas is the total energy of the ideal gas system. It includes the translational, rotational and vibrational kinetic energy of the gas.

$$
    U = K_{\textrm{trans}} + K_{\textrm{rot}} + K_{\textrm{vib}}
$$

The internal energy, derived from the equipartition theorem, is given by

$$
    \boxed{U = \frac{f}{2} N k_B T = \frac{f}{2} n R T}
$$

where

- $f$ is the number of degrees of freedom of the gas
- $N$ is the total number of molecules of the gas
- $n$ is the number of moles of the gas
- $T$ is the temperature of the gas

## Mean Free Path

Mean free path is the average distance a particle travels between consequetive collisions with other particles.

For a Maxwellian gas, the mean free path is given by

$$
    \boxed{\lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} n \sigma}}
$$

where $n = \dfrac{N}{V}$ is the number density of the target particles and $\sigma$ is the cross-sectional area for collision.

For hard-sphere molecules of diameter $d$, the mean free path is

$$
    \lambda = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 n} = \frac{k_B T}{\sqrt{2} \pi d^2 P}
$$
