## Postulates of Quantum Mechanics

- **State Postulate:** The state of an isolated quantum system is represented, at a fixed time $t$, by a state vector $\ket{\Psi}$ belonging to a Hilbert space $\mathcal H$ called the state space.

    The Hilbert space of a composite system is the Hilbert space tensor product of the state spaces associated with the component systems.

- **Measurement Postulate:** Every measurable quantity $ \mathcal A $ is described by a Hermitian operator $ \hat A $ acting in the state space $ \mathcal H $. This operator is an observable, meaning that its eigenstates form a basis for $ \mathcal H $. The result of measuring a physical quantity $ \mathcal A $ must be an eigenvalue of the corresponding observable $ \hat A $.

    When the physical quantity $\mathcal A$ is measured on a system in a normalized state $\ket{\Psi}$, the probability of obtaining an eigenvalue (denoted $a_n$ for discrete spectra and $\alpha$ for continuous spectra) of the corresponding observable $\hat A$ is given by the amplitude squared of the appropriate wave function (projection onto the corresponding eigenstate). This is known as the Born rule.
    
    $$
        P(a_n) = \lvert \braket{a_n | \Psi} \rvert^2 \quad \text{(For discrete spectra)}
    $$
    
    $$
        \mathrm d P(a_n) = \lvert \braket{\alpha | \Psi} \rvert^2 \, \mathrm d \alpha \quad \text{(For continuous spectra)}
    $$

    If the measurement of the physical quantity $\mathcal A$ on the system $ \ket{\Psi} $ gives the result $a_n$, then the state of the system immediately after the measurement is the normalized projection of $ \ket{\Psi} $ onto the eigensubspace associated with $a_n$.
    
    $$
        \ket{\Psi} \overset{ {a_n}{\longmapsto} } \dfrac{ \hat{P}_n \ket{\Psi} }{ \sqrt{ \braket{\Psi | \hat{P}_n | \Psi} } }
    $$

- **Evolution Postulate:** The time evolution of the state vector $ \ket{\Psi} $ is governed by the Schrödinger equation:

    $$
        i \hbar \dfrac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t} \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat H(t) \ket{\Psi(t)}
    $$
    
    where $\hat H (t)$ is the observable associated with the total energy of the system (called the Hamiltonian).

    Or, equivalently, the time evolution of a closed system is described by a unitary transformation on the initial state.
    
    $$
        \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat U (t; t_0)\ket{\Psi(t)}
    $$
    
    $t_0$ is often chosen to be zero for convenience.
    
    $$
        \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat U (t)\ket{\Psi(t)}
    $$

## State Space

State space is the Hilbert space of quantum states.

For one quantum particle, the space is $ \mathcal H $.

For two quantum particles with individual state spaces, $ \mathcal H_1 $ and $ \mathcal H_2 $, the composite system space is

$$
    \mathcal H = \mathcal H_1 \otimes \mathcal H_1
$$

For N quantum particles with individual state spaces $ \mathcal H_i $, the composite system space is

$$
    \mathcal H = \bigotimes_{i = 1}^N \mathcal H_i
$$

where $ \otimes $ denotes tensor product.

We use finite-dimensional Hilbert spaces $ (\mathbb R^n, \mathbb C^n ) $ for discrete state spaces and infinite-dimensional Hilbert spaces $ \left( L^2 (\mathbb R^n), L^2(\mathbb C^n) \right) $ for continuous state spaces.

## Quantum State

Quantum state is the description of a quantum system.

$$ 
    \ket{\Psi}
$$

Quantum state is represented by a ket (state vector).

### Stationary State

Stationary state is a quantum state whose observables are time-independent.

At a fixed time $t_0$, the time-dependent state $\ket{\Psi(t)}$ of a quantum system is the stationary state $\ket{\Psi(t_0)}$. $t_0$ is often taken to be zero, so the initial state of the system is $\ket{\Psi(0)}$

$$
    \ket{\Psi} \equiv \ket{\Psi(t_0)}
$$

They are solutions to the time-independent Schrödinger equation.

### Time-dependent State

Time-dependent state is a quantum state whose observables are time-dependent.

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)}
$$

They are solutions to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.

### Dual State

A dual state of the corresponding quantum state is represented by a bra.

$$
    \bra{\Psi (t)} = \ket{\Psi(t)}^{\dagger}
$$

## Observable

An observable is a linear Hermitian (or self-adjoint) operator $\hat A$ that performs measurement on the states of a quantum system.

**Properties:**

- **Eigenstate:** Eigenstates are definite states $\ket{a_n}$ or $\ket{\alpha}$ of the observable that the quantum must have.

    They are eigenvectors or eigenkets of the observable; they form a basis for the quantum system and are usually orthonormal.

    $$
        \hat A \ket{a_n} = a_n \ket{a_n} \quad \text{or} \quad \hat A \ket{\alpha} = \alpha \ket{\alpha}
    $$

- **Spectrum:** A spectrum is a set of probability amplitudes $a_n$ or $\alpha$ for each eigenstate that the system could have.

    It is the set of eigenvalues for corresponding eigenstates of the obserable, which creates a probability distribution for the observable quantity.

- **Statistical Measures:** Observables can also be treated as random variables for statistical analysis.

    $$
        \braket{\hat A} = \braket{\Psi | \hat A | \Psi}
    $$

    where $\braket{\hat A}$ is the expected value of $\hat A$

## Normalization Condition

For any state $\ket{\Psi}$,

$$
    \Vert \Psi \Vert^2 = \braket{\Psi | \Psi} = 1
$$

## Born Rule

The measurement of an observable $\hat A$ on a state $\ket{\Psi}$ of a quantum system will be an eigenvalue $a_n$ for discrete spectra or $\alpha_n$ for continuous spectra. The probability of the measurement is given by

$$
    \braket{\hat P_n} = \braket{\Psi | \hat P_n | \Psi}
$$

where $\hat P_n$ is the projection operator onto the eigenspace of $\hat A$ corresponding to the eigenvalue $a_n$ or $\alpha_n$.

For discrete spectra,

$$
    \hat P_n = \ket{a_n} \bra{a_n} \implies \braket{\hat P_n} = P(a_n) = \lvert \braket{a_n | \Psi} \rvert^2
$$

For continuous spectra,

$$
    \hat P_n = \ket{\alpha_n} \bra{\alpha_n} \implies \braket{\hat P_n} = P(\alpha_n) = \lvert \braket{\alpha_n | \Psi} \rvert^2
$$

## Dynamical Pictures

Quantum mechanics can be represented by two pictures or representations:

- Schrödinger Picture (Wave Mechanical Picture / Active Picture)
- Heisenberg Picture (Matrix Mechanical Mechanical Picture / Passive Picture)

Both pictures are equivalent and are related by the time evolution operator $\hat U(t)$.

### Schrödinger Picture

In this picture, the state of the system is allowed to evolve but the observables are kept constant.

State evolution: $\ket{\Psi_S(t)} = \hat U(t) \ket{\Psi_S(0)} = \hat U(t) \ket{\Psi_H}$

Observable evolution: Constant $\left(\hat A_S \equiv \hat A_H (0)\right)$

### Heisenberg Picture

In this picture, the state of the system is kept constant but the observables are allowed to evolve in time.

State evolution: Constant $\left( \ket{\Psi_H} \equiv \ket{\Psi_S (0)} \right)$

Observable evolution: $\hat A_H (t) = \hat U^{\dagger} (t) \hat A_H (0) U(t) = \hat U^{\dagger} (t) \hat A_S U(t)$

## Probability Amplitude

Probability amplitudes are the expansion coefficients of eigenstates of observables that have a discrete spectrum.

For a state $\ket{\Psi}$ or $\ket{\Psi(t)}$, if the obserable $\hat A$ has discrete eigenstates $\ket{a_i}$ with eigenvalues $a_i$, then the probability amplitude in the eigensubspace of $a_i$ ($a$-space) is

$$
    \Psi(t) = \braket{a_k | \Psi(t)}
$$

where the eigenstates are orthonormal. That is,

$$
    \braket{a_i | a_j} = \delta_{ij}
$$

An operator $\hat A$ acting on probability amplitude $\Psi_k(t)$ is defined as

$$
    \hat A \Psi_k(t) = \braket{a_k | \hat A | \Psi (t)}
$$

The state can be expressed in a superposition of eigenstates $\ket{a_i}$ of $\hat A$.

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \sum_i \Psi_i (t) \ket{a_i}
$$

The inner product for probability amplitudes is given by

$$
    \braket{\Phi (t) | \Psi (t)} = \sum_i \Phi_i^* (t) \Psi_i(t)
$$

For probability amplitudes, the normalization condition is

$$
    \Vert \Psi (t) \Vert = 1 \implies \sum_i | \Psi(t) |^2 = 1
$$

Probability of having an eigenstate $\ket{a_n}$ at time $t$ is

$$
    P(a_n, t) = | \Psi_n (t) |^2
$$

### Spin Amplitude

For a Fermion, spin quantum number $s = \frac{1}{2}$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\uparrow} \equiv \ket{\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}}, \ket{\downarrow} \equiv \ket{\frac{1}{2}, -\frac{1}{2}}$

Eigenvalues: $\hbar m_s$

The equations for the spin amplitudes are the following:

$$
    \Psi_{\uparrow}(t) = \braket{\uparrow | \Psi(t)}, \Psi_{\downarrow}(t) = \braket{\downarrow | \Psi(t)}
$$

$$
    \braket{\uparrow | \uparrow} = 1, \braket{\downarrow | \downarrow} = 1, \braket{\uparrow | \downarrow} = \braket{\downarrow | \uparrow} = 0
$$

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \Psi_{\uparrow}(t) \ket{\uparrow} + \Psi_{\downarrow}(t) \ket{\downarrow}
$$

$$
    \braket{\Phi(t) | \Psi(t)} = \Phi^*_{\uparrow}(t) \Psi_{\uparrow}(t) + \Phi^*_{\downarrow}(t) \Psi_{\downarrow}(t)
$$

$$
    |\Psi_{\uparrow}(t)|^2 + |\Psi_{\downarrow}(t)|^2 = 1
$$

$$
    P(\uparrow, t) = |\Psi_{\uparrow}(t)|^2, P(\downarrow, t) = |\Psi_{\downarrow}(t)|^2
$$


## Wave Function

Wave function of a quantum system is the probability amplitude function of observables that have a continuous spectrum.

For a state $\ket{\Psi}$ or $\ket{\Psi(t)}$, if the obserable $\hat A$ has continuous eigenstates $\ket{\alpha}$ with eigenvalues $\alpha$, then the wave function in the eigensubspace of $\alpha$ ($\alpha$-space) is

$$
    \Psi(\alpha, t) = \braket{\alpha | \Psi(t)}
$$

where the eigenstates $\ket{\alpha}$ are orthonormal. That is,

$$
    \braket{\alpha | \alpha^{\prime}} = \delta(\alpha - \alpha^{\prime})
$$

An operator $\hat A$ acting on wave function $\Psi(\alpha, t)$ is defined as

$$
    \hat A \Psi(\alpha, t) = \braket{\alpha | \hat A | \Psi (t)}
$$

The state can be expressed in a superposition of eigenstates $\ket{\alpha}$ of $\hat A$.

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \int \mathrm d \alpha \, \Psi (\alpha, t) \ket{\alpha}
$$

The inner product for wave functions is given by

$$
    \braket{\Phi (t) | \Psi (t)} = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm d \alpha \, \Phi^*(\alpha, t) \Psi(\alpha, t)
$$

For wave functions, the normalization condition is

$$
    \Vert \Psi (t) \Vert = 1 \implies \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \mathrm d \alpha \, | \Psi (\alpha, t) |^2 = 1
$$

So the probability density for wave functions is given by

$$
    \rho(\alpha, t) = |\Psi (\alpha, t)|^2
$$

Probability of having an eigenstate between $\ket{\beta}$ and $\ket{\gamma}$ is

$$
    P(\beta \leq \alpha \leq \gamma, t) = \int_{\alpha}^{\gamma} \mathrm d \alpha \, |\Psi (\alpha, t)|^2
$$

The above equations take similar forms for time-independent states $\ket{\Psi}$.

### Position Wave Function

A commonly used wave function is the position wave function $\ket{\Psi(\mathbf r, t)}$, which is expressed in the position space. This is formally known as the position representation of the wave function.

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf r} = (\ket{x}, \ket{y}, \ket{z})$

Eigenvalues: $\mathbf r$

The equations for the position wavefunction are the following:

$$
    \Psi (\mathbf r, t) = \braket{\mathbf r | \Psi (t)}
$$

$$
    \braket{\mathbf r | \mathbf r^{\prime}} = \delta(\mathbf r - \mathbf r^{\prime})
$$

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \int \mathrm d \mathbf r \, \Psi(\mathbf r, t) \ket{\mathbf r}
$$

$$
    \braket{\Phi (t) | \Psi (t)} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf r \, \Phi^* (\mathbf r, t) \Psi (\mathbf r, t)
$$

$$
    \Vert \Psi (t) \Vert = 1 \implies \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf r \, | \Psi (\mathbf r, t) |^2 = 1
$$

$$
    \rho(\mathbf r) = |\Psi (\mathbf r, t)|^2
$$

$$
    P\left(\mathbf r \in D\right) = \int_D \mathrm d \mathbf r |\Psi (\mathbf r, t)|^2
$$

### Momentum Wave Function

The momentum wave function $\ket{\Phi(\mathbf p, t)}$ is expressed in the momentum space. This is formally known as the momentum representation of the wave function.

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf p} = (\ket{p_x}, \ket{p_y}, \ket{p_z})$

Eigenvalues: $\mathbf p$

The equations for the position wavefunction are the following:

$$
    \Phi(\mathbf p, t) = \braket{\mathbf p | \Phi (t)}
$$

$$
    \braket{\mathbf p | \mathbf p^{\prime}} = \delta(\mathbf p - \mathbf p^{\prime})
$$

$$
    \ket{\Phi(t)} = \int \mathrm d \mathbf p \, \Phi(\mathbf p, t) \ket{\mathbf p}
$$

$$
    \braket{\Psi (t) | \Phi (t)} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf p \, \Psi^*(\mathbf p, t) \Phi(\mathbf p, t)
$$

$$
    \Vert \Phi(t) \Vert = 1 \implies \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf p \, | \Phi(\mathbf p, t) |^2 = 1
$$

$$
    \rho(\mathbf p) = |\Phi(\mathbf p, t)|^2
$$

$$
    P\left(\mathbf p \in D\right) = \int_D \mathrm d \mathbf p |\Phi(\mathbf p, t)|^2
$$

### Change of Representation

In quantum mechanics, position and momentum are conjugate quantities. So we can switch between the representations using Fourier transformations.

$$
    \braket{\mathbf r | \mathbf p} = \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} \right)^3 \exp{\left( i \dfrac{\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf p}{\hbar} \right)}
$$

$$
    \implies \ket{\mathbf p} = \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} \right)^3 \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf r \, \exp{\left( i \dfrac{\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf p}{\hbar} \right)} \ket{\mathbf r}
$$

$$
    \implies \ket{\mathbf r} = \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} \right)^3 \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf p \, \exp{\left( -i \dfrac{\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf p}{\hbar} \right)} \ket{\mathbf p}
$$

We can also change representations for wave functions in the following way:

$$
    \Phi(\mathbf p, t) = \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} \right)^3 \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf r \, \exp{\left( i \dfrac{\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf p}{\hbar} \right)} \Psi(\mathbf r, t)
$$

$$
    \Psi(\mathbf r, t) = \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \hbar}} \right)^3 \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathrm d \mathbf p \, \exp{\left( i \dfrac{\mathbf r \cdot \mathbf p}{\hbar} \right)} \Phi(\mathbf p, t)
$$
