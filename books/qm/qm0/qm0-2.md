## Position Operator

### Position Operator in Position Space

In position space, the position operator is defined as

$$
    \hat{\mathbf r} = \mathbf r
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf r}$

Eigenvalues: $\mathbf r$

$\hat{\mathbf r}$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{\mathbf r} \ket{\mathbf r} = \mathbf r \ket{\mathbf r} 
    \implies \hat{\mathbf r} \Psi(\mathbf r, t) = \mathbf r \Psi(\mathbf r, t)
$$

Expected value,

$$
    \braket{\hat{\mathbf r}} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathbf r \left| \Psi(\mathbf r, t) \right|^2 \, \mathrm d^3 \mathbf r
$$

### Position Operator in Momentum Space

In position space, the position operator is defined as

$$
    \hat{\mathbf r} = i \hbar \dfrac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf p}
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf p}$

Eigenvalues: $i \hbar \dfrac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf p}$

$\hat{\mathbf r}$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{\mathbf r} \ket{\mathbf p} = i \hbar \dfrac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf p} \ket{\mathbf p} 
    \implies \hat{\mathbf r} \Phi(\mathbf p, t) = i \hbar \dfrac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf p} \Phi(\mathbf p, t)
$$

Expected value,

$$
    \braket{\hat{\mathbf r}} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \Phi^*(\mathbf p, t) \left( i \hbar \dfrac{\partial}{\partial \mathbf p} \right) \Phi(\mathbf p, t) \, \mathrm d^3 \mathbf p
$$

## Momentum Operator

### Momentum Operator in Position Space

In position space, the momentum operator is defined as

$$
    \hat{\mathbf p} = -i \hbar \nabla
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf r}$

Eigenvalues: $-i \hbar \nabla$

$\hat{\mathbf p}$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{\mathbf p} \ket{\mathbf r} = -i \hbar \nabla \ket{\mathbf r} 
    \implies \hat{\mathbf p} \Psi(\mathbf r, t) = -i \hbar \nabla \Psi(\mathbf r, t)
$$

Expected value,

$$
    \braket{\hat{\mathbf p}} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \Psi^*(\mathbf r, t) \left( -i \hbar \nabla \right)\Psi(\mathbf r, t) \, \mathrm d^3 \mathbf r
$$

### Momentum Operator in Momentum Space

In momentum space, the momentum operator is defined as

$$
    \hat{\mathbf p} = \mathbf p
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\mathbf p}$

Eigenvalues: $\mathbf p$

$\hat{\mathbf p}$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{\mathbf p} \ket{\mathbf p} = \mathbf p \ket{\mathbf p} 
    \implies \hat{\mathbf p} \Phi(\mathbf p, t) = \mathbf p \Phi(\mathbf p, t)
$$

Expected value,

$$
    \braket{\hat{\mathbf p}} = \int_{\mathbb R^3} \mathbf p \left| \Phi(\mathbf p, t) \right|^2 \, \mathrm d^3 \mathbf p
$$

## Hamiltonian Operator

### Hamiltonian Operator for One Particle

For one particle, the Hamiltonian operator is

$$
    \hat{H}(t) = \hat{T} + \hat{V}(t) = - \dfrac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla^2 + V(\mathbf r, t)
$$

where $\hat{T}$ is the kinetic energy operator and $\hat{V}(t)$ is the potential energy operator. They are given by

$$
    \hat{T} = \dfrac{\mathbf p \cdot \mathbf p}{2m} = - \dfrac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla^2 
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \hat{V}(t) = V(\mathbf r, t)
$$

### Hamiltonian Operator for Multiple Particles

For $N$ particles, the Hamiltonian operator is

$$
    \hat{H}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^N \hat{T}_i + \hat{V}(t) = - \sum_{i=1}^N \dfrac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla_i^2 + V(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_N, t)
$$

where $\hat{T}$ is the kinetic energy operator for each particle and $\hat{V}(t)$ is the potential energy operator of the system. They are given by

$$
    \hat{T}_i = \dfrac{\mathbf p_i \cdot \mathbf p_i}{2m} = - \dfrac{\hbar}{2m} \nabla_i^2 
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \hat{V}(t) = V(\mathbf r_1, \dots, \mathbf r_N, t)
$$

If the particles are non-interacting, then

$$
    \hat{V}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^N V_i(\mathbf r_i, t) \implies \hat{H}(t) = \sum_{i=1}^N \hat{H}_i (t)
$$

where $\hat{H}_i (t)$ is the Hamiltonian operator for the $i$-th particle.

The Hamiltonian operator is time-independent when the potential energy operator is time-independent.

## Time Evolution

If the Hamiltonian operator is time-independent, then the time evolution of a quantum state from an initial time $t_0$ to an arbitrary time $t$ is given by the unitary time evolution operator.

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{U}(t; t_0) \ket{\Psi(t_0)}
$$

Usually, $t_0$ is taken to be zero.

$$
    \ket{\Psi(t)} = \hat{U}(t) \ket{\Psi(0)}
$$

$$
    \implies \hat{U}(t) = \exp{\left( -i \dfrac{\hat{H}}{\hbar} t \right)}
$$

## Angular Momentum Operator

### Spin Angular Momentum Operator

The spin angular momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf S} = \left( \hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z \right)$ represents a particle's intrinsic angular momentum in quantum mechanics.

We usually only use the $z$-component of the operator, $\hat S_z$. The quantum number associated with $\hat S_z$ is the spin magnetic quantum number for $z$-axis, $m_s \equiv m_{sz}$. The spin quantum number of the particle is $s$.

$$
    \hat{S}_z = \hbar m_s
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{s, m_s}$

Eigenvalues: $\hbar m_s$

$s_z = \hbar m_s$ is known as the $z$-axis projection value of the spin quantum number.

$\hat{S}_z$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{S}_z \ket{s, m_s} = \hbar m_s \ket{s, m_s}
$$

There is another operator, called the total spin angular momentum operator $\hat{S}^2$, which is given by

$$
    \hat{S}^2 = \hat{S}_x^2 + \hat{S}_y^2 + \hat{S}_z^2 = \hbar^2 s(s + 1)
$$

$\hat{S}^2$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{S}^2 \ket{s, m_s} = \hbar^2 s(s + 1) \ket{s, m_s}
$$

The operators obey the following commutation relations:

$$
    \left[ \hat{S}_a, \hat{S}_b \right] = i \hbar \varepsilon_{abc} \hat{S}_c
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \left[ \hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_i \right] = 0
$$

which imply that

$$
    \left[ \hat{S}_x, \hat{S}_y \right] = i \hbar \hat{S}_z, \quad
    \left[ \hat{S}_y, \hat{S}_z \right] = i \hbar \hat{S}_x, \quad
    \left[ \hat{S}_z, \hat{S}_x \right] = i \hbar \hat{S}_y
$$

$$
    \left[ \hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_x \right] = \left[ \hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_y \right] = \left[ \hat{S}^2, \hat{S}_z \right] = 0
$$

**Example:**

For a fermion, $s = \frac{1}{2}$ and $m_s = \frac{1}{2}, - \frac{1}{2}$

Eigenstates: $\ket{\uparrow} \equiv \ket{\frac{1}{2}, \frac{1}{2}}$, $\ket{\downarrow} \equiv \ket{\frac{1}{2}, - \frac{1}{2}}$

Eigenvalues of $\hat{S}_z$: $\frac{\hbar}{2}$ for $\ket{\uparrow}$, $-\frac{\hbar}{2}$ for $\ket{\downarrow}$

Eigenvalues of $\hat{S}^2$: $\frac{3}{4} \hbar^2$

If $\ket{\uparrow}$ $\ket{\downarrow}$ are represented by column vectors, then

$$
    \hat{\mathbf S} = \dfrac{\hbar}{2} \boldsymbol \sigma \implies
    \hat{S}_x = \dfrac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_x, \quad
    \hat{S}_y = \dfrac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_y, \quad
    \hat{S}_z = \dfrac{\hbar}{2} \sigma_z
$$

where $\sigma_x$, $\sigma_y$ and $\sigma_z$ are Pauli matrices.

### Orbital Angular Momentum Operator

The orbital angular momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf L} = \left( \hat{L}_x, \hat{L}_y, \hat{L}_z \right)$ represents a particle's extrinsic angular momentum in quantum mechanics.

We usually only use the $z$-component of the operator, $\hat L_z$. The quantum number associated with $\hat L_z$ is the orbital magnetic quantum number for $z$-axis, $m_l \equiv m_{lz}$. The orbital quantum number of the particle is $l$.

$$
    \hat{L}_z = \hbar m_l
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{l, m_l}$

Eigenvalues: $\hbar m_l$

$l_z = \hbar m_l$ is known as the $z$-axis projection value of the orbital quantum number.

$\hat{L}_z$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{L}_z \ket{l, m_l} = \hbar m_l \ket{l, m_l}
$$

There is another operator, called the total orbital angular momentum operator $\hat{L}^2$, which is given by

$$
    \hat{L}^2 = \hat{L}_x^2 + \hat{L}_y^2 + \hat{L}_z^2 = \hbar^2 l(l + 1)
$$

$\hat{L}^2$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{L}^2 \ket{l, m_l} = \hbar^2 l(l + 1) \ket{l, m_l}
$$

The operators obey the following commutation relations:

$$
    \left[ \hat{L}_a, \hat{L}_b \right] = i \hbar \varepsilon_{abc} \hat{L}_c
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \left[ \hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_i \right] = 0
$$

which imply that

$$
    \left[ \hat{L}_x, \hat{L}_y \right] = i \hbar \hat{L}_z, \quad
    \left[ \hat{L}_y, \hat{L}_z \right] = i \hbar \hat{L}_x, \quad
    \left[ \hat{L}_z, \hat{L}_x \right] = i \hbar \hat{L}_y
$$

$$
    \left[ \hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_x \right] = \left[ \hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_y \right] = \left[ \hat{L}^2, \hat{L}_z \right] = 0
$$

For position space, the orbital angular momentum operator can also be expressed as

$$
    \hat{\mathbf L} = \hat{\mathbf r} \times \hat{\mathbf p} = -i \hbar (\mathbf r \times \nabla)
$$

$$
    \implies \hat{L}_x = \hat{y} \hat{p}_z - \hat{z} \hat{p}_y, \quad
    \hat{L}_y = \hat{z} \hat{p}_x - \hat{x} \hat{p}_z, \quad
    \hat{L}_z = \hat{x} \hat{p}_y - \hat{y} \hat{p}_x, \quad
$$

### Total Angular Momentum Operator

The total angular momentum operator $\hat{\mathbf J} = \left( \hat{J}_x, \hat{J}_y, \hat{J}_z \right)$ combines a particle's intrinsic and extrinsic angular momentum.

We usually only use the $z$-component of the operator, $\hat J_z$. The quantum number associated with $\hat J_z$ is $m_j \equiv m_{jz}$. The total angular momentum quantum number of the particle is $j$.

$$
    \hat{J}_z = \hbar m_j
$$

Eigenstates: $\ket{j, m_j}$

Eigenvalues: $\hbar m_j$

$j_z = \hbar m_j$ is known as the $z$-axis projection value of the orbital quantum number.

$\hat{J}_z$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{J}_z \ket{j, m_j} = \hbar m_j \ket{j, m_j}
$$

There is another operator $\hat{J}^2$, which is given by

$$
    \hat{J}^2 = \hat{J}_x^2 + \hat{J}_y^2 + \hat{J}_z^2 = \hbar^2 j(j + 1)
$$

$\hat{J}^2$ satisfies

$$
    \hat{J}^2 \ket{j, m_j} = \hbar^2 j(j + 1) \ket{j, m_j}
$$

The operators obey the following commutation relations:

$$
    \left[ \hat{J}_a, \hat{J}_b \right] = i \hbar \varepsilon_{abc} \hat{J}_c
    \quad \text{and} \quad
    \left[ \hat{J}^2, \hat{J}_i \right] = 0
$$

which imply that

$$
    \left[ \hat{J}_x, \hat{J}_y \right] = i \hbar \hat{J}_z, \quad
    \left[ \hat{J}_y, \hat{J}_z \right] = i \hbar \hat{J}_x, \quad
    \left[ \hat{J}_z, \hat{J}_x \right] = i \hbar \hat{J}_y
$$

$$
    \left[ \hat{J}^2, \hat{J}_x \right] = \left[ \hat{J}^2, \hat{J}_y \right] = \left[ \hat{J}^2, \hat{J}_z \right] = 0
$$

## Translation Operator

Translation operator is a unitary operator that translates a state by distance $\ket{\mathbf x}$.

$$
    \hat{T}(\mathbf x) = \exp{\left( -i \dfrac{\hat{\mathbf x} \cdot \hat{\mathbf p}}{\hbar} \right)}
$$

$$
    \implies \hat{T}(\mathbf x) \ket{\mathbf r} = \ket{\mathbf r} + \ket{\mathbf x}, \quad
    \hat{T}(\mathbf x) \Psi(\mathbf r, t) = \Psi(\mathbf r - \mathbf x, t)
$$

## Rotation Operator

The rotation operator rotates a state by angle $\theta$ about the $\hat{\mathbf n}$ axis.

$$
    \hat{R}(\hat{\mathbf n}, \theta) = \exp{\left( -i \dfrac{\theta \hat{\mathbf n} \cdot \hat{\mathbf J}}{\hbar} \right)}
$$

where $\hat{\mathbf J}$ is the total angular momentum operator.
