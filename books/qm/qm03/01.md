# Section 1

# Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

The quantum mechanical model is our current model of the atom. It is based on the principles of quantum mechanics and is primarily derived from the Schrödinger equation. The core features of this model are:

- Electrons exist as standing matter waves around the nucleus of an atom and is described by a wave function $\Psi(\mathbf r, t)$.

- It is not possible to definitely know both the position and energy of an electron. The probability density $\lvert \Psi(\mathbf r, t) \rvert^2$ gives the probability of finding an electron in a given region within the atom.

- An electron mostly exists in orbitals. An orbital is a region within an atom where the probability of finding the electron is 90%.

- An electron can have one of two possible spin values: spin-up or spin-down.

- Any two electrons occupying the same orbital must have opposite spins.

## Hydrogen Atom

The time-independent Schrödinger equation for the hydrogen atom is

$$
    \left[ - \dfrac{\hbar^2}{2 \mu} \nabla^2 - \dfrac{e^2}{4\pi \varepsilon_0 r} \right] \psi(\mathbf r) = E \psi(\mathbf r)
$$

where $\mu = \frac{m_e m_p}{m_e + m_p}$ is the reduced mass of the electron-proton system.

The hydrogen atom is spherically symmetric, so it is convenient to solve the equation in spherical coordinates.

$$
    \psi(\mathbf r) = \psi(r, \theta, \varphi)
$$

The equation can be solved in terms of special functions. The solutions, which are normalized position wave functions, are:

$$
    \psi_{nlm}(r, \theta, \varphi) = \sqrt{\left( \dfrac{2}{na_0^*} \right)^3 \dfrac{(n - l - 1)!}{2n(n + l)!}} e^{-\rho / 2 } \rho^l L_{n - l - 1}^{2l + 1}(\rho) Y_l^m(\theta, \varphi)
$$

where:

- $\rho$ is a constant, $\rho = \frac{2r}{na_0^*}$,

- $a_0^*$ is the reduced Bohr radius, $a_0^{\*} = \frac{4\pi \varepsilon_0 \hbar^2}{\mu e^2}$,

- $n$, $l$ and $m$ are quantum numbers.

- $L_{n - l - 1}^{2l + 1}(\rho)$ is a generalized Laguerre polynomial of degree $n - l - 1$, and

- $Y_l^m(\theta, \varphi)$ is a spherical harmonic function of degree $l$ and order $m$.

### Quantum Numbers

The finite and normalizable solutions only exist for discrete values of the quantum numbers $n$, $l$ amd $m$. After the discovery of electron spin, a fourth quantum number was added to the model. So there are 4 quantum numbers for this model: $n$, $l$, $m_l$, $m_s$

- **Principal Quantum Number** $(n)$: Corresponds to shells (energy levels) in the atom.

    $$
        n \in \mathbb N \implies n = 1, 2, 3, \dots
    $$

- **Azimuthal Quantum Number** $(l)$: Corresponds to subshells and orbital angular momentum of the electron inside the atom.

    $$
        l \in \{ l \in \mathbb Z \, | \, 0 < l < (n - 1) \} \implies l = 0, 1, 2, \ldots , (n - 1)
    $$

    The orbital angular momentum is quantized.

    $$
        L = \hbar \sqrt{l(l + 1)}
    $$

- **Orbital Magnetic Quantum Number** $(m_l)$: Corresponds to orbitals and the projection of the orbital angular momentum of the electron along a certain axis (typically the $z$-axis).

    $$
        m_l \in \{ m_l \in \mathbb Z \, | \, -l < m_l < l \} \implies m_l = -l, \ldots, l
    $$

    The $z$-component of orbital angular momentum is given by

    $$
        L_z = \hbar m_{lz}
    $$

    where $m_{lz}$ is the value of $m_l$ for $z$-axis.

- **Spin Magnetic Quantum Number** $(m_s)$: Corresponds to orbital orientation and the projection of the spin angular momentum of the electron along a certain axis (typically the $z$-axis).

    $$
        m_s \in \{ m_s \in \mathbb Z \, | \, -s \leq m_s \leq s \} \implies m_s = -s, \ldots, s
    $$

    The $z$-component of spin angular momentum is given by

    $$
        S_z = \hbar m_{sz}
    $$

    where $m_{sz}$ is the value of $m_s$ for $z$-axis.

    For electrons,

    $$
        s = \dfrac{1}{2} \implies m_s = \pm \dfrac{1}{2} \implies S_z = \pm \dfrac{\hbar}{2}
    $$

### Ground State

The ground state of the hydrogen atom is found for the $1s$ orbital $(n = 1, l = 0, m = 0)$.

$$
    \psi_{1s} (r) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{\pi a_0^3}} e^{-r/a_0}
$$

where $a_0$ is the Bohr radius.

The probability distribution for the ground state is given by

$$
    P(r) = 4\pi r^2 \left| \psi_{1s} (r) \right|^2 = \dfrac{4 r^2}{a_0^3} e^{-2r/a_0}
$$

which has a maximum at $r = a_0$. Therefore, the most probable distance of the electron from the nucleus in the hydrogen ground state is one Bohr radius.

### Energy Levels

Electrons cannot have arbitrary energy and can only occupy discrete energy states, given by $E_n$

$$
    E_n = - \dfrac{R_{\infty} hc}{n^2} = -\dfrac{m_e e^4}{8 \varepsilon_0 h^2} \dfrac{1}{n^2} \approx - \dfrac{13.6 \, \mathrm{eV}}{n^2}
$$

where $R_{\infty}$ is the Rydberg constant.

### Atomic Spectral Lines

An electron can transition between different energy levels by absorbing or emitting energy. The emitted energy is observed as spectral lines from the atom. The emitted or absorbed energy is given by

$$
    \Delta E = E_f - E_i = h \nu \implies \dfrac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \dfrac{1}{n_f^2} - \dfrac{1}{n_i^2} \right)
$$

where $R_H$ is the Rydberg constant for hydrogen and $\lambda$ is the wavelength of the emitted or absorbed photon.

### Orbitals and Nodes

Orbitals are regions within an atom where the probability of the electron existing is 90%. Each orbital can contain at most 2 electrons of opposite spins.

There are $2l + 1$ orbitals for each subshell (given by $l$). Each energy level or shell (given by $n$) has $n^2$ orbitals

Nodes are regions where the wave function is exactly zero.

There are $l$ angular nodes ($m_l$ around the $\varphi$-axis and $l - m_l$ on the $\theta$-axis) and $n - l - 1$ radial nodes in an atom. Thus, the total number of nodes in an atom is $n - 1$.
