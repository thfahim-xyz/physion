# Reflection and Refraction

## Optical Ray

An optical ray is the idealized path of light in the geometrical-optics limit, where the wavelength is negligible compared with apertures and inhomogeneity scales $(\lambda\to 0)$. It is normal to the wavefront at each point.

In a homogeneous medium the ray is straight; at an interface or in a gradient-index medium it bends according to Fermat's principle.

**Limitation:** the ray picture fails at foci, caustics, and apertures comparable to $\lambda$, where diffraction must be used.

## Fermat's Principle

**Principle:** the physical ray between two points renders the optical path stationary:

$$
    \boxed{\delta L=0}
$$

where the optical path length is:

$$
    \boxed{L=\int_A^B n\,\mathrm d s}
$$

with $n$ the refractive index and $\mathrm d s$ the arc-length element. Transit time is $t=L/c$, so stationary $L$ is stationary time.

**Interpretation:** in uniform $n$, $\delta L=0$ gives a straight line; at interfaces it selects the reflection and refraction laws below.

**Remark:** stationary includes minima, and occasionally maxima or saddle paths; the minimum-time form suffices for the canonical derivations here.

## Law of Reflection

**Law:** at a smooth interface, the incident ray, reflected ray, and surface normal are coplanar, and:

$$
    \boxed{\theta_i=\theta_r}
$$

where angles are measured from the normal.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Let $A$ and $B$ lie in the same medium $n$ at heights $a,b$ above a mirror plane, separated horizontally by $d$. Let the reflection point be at horizontal coordinate $x$. Then:

$$L=n\left(\sqrt{x^2+a^2}+\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}\right)$$

$$\frac{\mathrm d L}{\mathrm d x}=0\implies n\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}-n\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}}=0$$

$$\implies \sin\theta_i=\sin\theta_r\implies \boxed{\theta_i=\theta_r}$$

since $x/\sqrt{x^2+a^2}=\sin\theta_i$ and $(d-x)/\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}=\sin\theta_r$.

</div>

**Limitation:** ideal smooth interface; roughness produces diffuse reflection.

## Snell's Law

**Law:** at the interface of media $n_1,n_2$, the incident ray, refracted ray, and normal are coplanar, and:

$$
    \boxed{n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2}
$$

where $\theta_1,\theta_2$ are measured from the normal.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

With the same geometry but $A$ in $n_1$ and $B$ in $n_2$:

$$L=n_1\sqrt{x^2+a^2}+n_2\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}$$

$$\frac{\mathrm d L}{\mathrm d x}=0\implies n_1\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}}-n_2\frac{d-x}{\sqrt{(d-x)^2+b^2}}=0$$

$$\implies \boxed{n_1\sin\theta_1=n_2\sin\theta_2}$$

</div>

**Interpretation:** bending toward the normal occurs when $n_2 \gt n_1$; bending away occurs when $n_2 \lt n_1$.

## Refractive Index

**Definition:** for a non-magnetic optical medium with phase speed $v$:

$$
    \boxed{n=\frac{c}{v}}
$$

so $[n]=1$. In terms of relative permittivity and permeability, $n=\sqrt{\varepsilon_r\mu_r}$, approximately $\sqrt{\varepsilon_r}$ in optics.

The relative index from medium $1$ to medium $2$ is:

$$
    \boxed{n_{21}=\frac{n_2}{n_1}=\frac{v_1}{v_2}=\frac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2}}
$$

since frequency $f$ is fixed across the interface while $\lambda=v/f$ changes.

**Remark:** $n$ varies with wavelength (dispersion); tabulated values quote a reference wavelength. Detailed dispersion belongs to Optical Systems, not here.

## Critical Angle

**Definition:** for $n_1 \gt n_2$, the incidence angle whose refracted ray grazes the interface $(\theta_2=\pi/2)$:

$$
    \boxed{\sin\theta_c=\frac{n_2}{n_1}}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

From Snell's law, $n_1\sin\theta_c=n_2\sin(\pi/2)=n_2$.

For $\theta_1 \gt \theta_c$ no transmitted ray satisfies Snell's law; all energy is reflected. This is total internal reflection:

$$
    \boxed{\theta_1 \gt \theta_c\implies \text{total internal reflection}}
$$

</div>

The canonical application is light confinement in optical fibers and prisms.

**Limitation:** an evanescent wave still penetrates the rarer medium; the Fresnel amplitudes and polarization dependence belong to Polarization, not here.
