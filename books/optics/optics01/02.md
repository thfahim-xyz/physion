# Optical Systems

## Paraxial Approximation

Rays making small angles with the optical axis satisfy:

$$
    \boxed{\sin\theta\approx\tan\theta\approx\theta,\quad \cos\theta\approx 1}
$$

with $\theta$ in radians. All mirror and lens formulae below assume this limit, so that spherical surfaces focus paraxial rays stigmatically to first order.

**Limitation:** marginal rays violate the approximation and produce spherical aberration; exact ray tracing is then required.

## Optical Instruments

### Mirrors

Mirrors form images by reflection. In the paraxial taxonomy:

#### Plane Mirror

Flat reflecting surface. Forms a virtual image as far behind the mirror as the object is in front, with unit erect magnification.

| Object position | Image position | Size | Orientation | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Any $d_o \gt 0$ | $d_i=-d_o$ behind mirror | Same, $\lvert m \rvert =1$ | Erect | Virtual |

#### Convex Mirror

Outward-curving surface $(f \lt 0)$. Forms diminished, erect, virtual images over a wide field; hence its use in rear-view optics.

| Object position | Image position | Size | Orientation | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Any $d_o \gt 0$ | $-\lvert f \rvert \lt d_i \lt 0$, object side | Diminished, $\lvert m \rvert \lt 1$ | Erect | Virtual |

#### Concave Mirror

Inward-curving surface $(f \gt 0)$. Converges paraxial rays toward a real focus; position table with center $C=2f$:

| Object position | Image position | Size | Orientation | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $d_o\to\infty$ | $d_i=f$ | Point, $m\to 0$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o \gt 2f$ (beyond $C$) | $f \lt d_i \lt 2f$ | Diminished, $\lvert m \rvert \lt 1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o=2f$ (at $C$) | $d_i=2f$ | Same, $\lvert m \rvert =1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $f \lt d_o \lt 2f$ (between $F$ and $C$) | $d_i \gt 2f$ | Enlarged, $\lvert m \rvert \gt 1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o=f$ | $d_i\to\infty$ | Enlarged without bound | Inverted | Real (at infinity) |
| $d_o \lt f$ (inside $F$) | $d_i \lt 0$, object side | Enlarged, $\lvert m \rvert \gt 1$ | Erect | Virtual |

### Lenses

Lenses form images by refraction through two surfaces. In the paraxial taxonomy:

#### Diverging Lens

Thinner at the center $(f \lt 0)$. Spreads paraxial rays as if from a virtual focus on the object side.

| Object position | Image position | Size | Orientation | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Any $d_o \gt 0$ | $-\lvert f \rvert \lt d_i \lt 0$, object side | Diminished, $\lvert m \rvert \lt 1$ | Erect | Virtual |

#### Converging Lens

Thicker at the center $(f \gt 0)$. Brings paraxial rays toward a real focus on the transmitted side:

| Object position | Image position | Size | Orientation | Nature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $d_o\to\infty$ | $d_i=f$ | Point, $m\to 0$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o \gt 2f$ (beyond $2F$) | $f \lt d_i \lt 2f$ | Diminished, $\lvert m \rvert \lt 1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o=2f$ | $d_i=2f$ | Same, $\lvert m \rvert=1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $f \lt d_o \lt 2f$ (between $F$ and $2F$) | $d_i \gt 2f$ | Enlarged, $\lvert m \rvert \gt 1$ | Inverted | Real |
| $d_o=f$ | $d_i\to\infty$ | Enlarged without bound | Inverted | Real (at infinity) |
| $d_o \lt f$ (inside $F$) | $d_i \lt 0$, object side | Enlarged, $\lvert m \rvert \gt 1$ | Erect | Virtual |

## Mirror Equation

**Result:** for a spherical mirror of radius of curvature $R$ in the paraxial limit:

$$
    \boxed{\frac1f=\frac1{d_o}+\frac1{d_i}},\quad \boxed{f=\frac{R}{2}}
$$

where $d_o$ is the object distance, $d_i$ the image distance, and $R$ is positive for a concave mirror facing the incident light under the Cartesian convention stated below.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

An axial object point sends a ray striking the mirror at small height $h$. The local normal tilts by $h/R$, so reflection deflects the ray by $2h/R$ toward the axis. The incident slope is $h/d_o$ and the reflected slope is $-h/d_i$; geometry gives $h/d_o+h/d_i=2h/R$, whence the equation with $f=R/2$.

</div>

**Limit:** $R\to\infty$ gives the plane mirror, $d_i=-d_o$.

## Lens Equation

### Thin Lens Equation

**Result:** for a thin lens of focal length $f$ in air:

$$
    \boxed{\frac1f=\frac1{d_o}+\frac1{d_i}}
$$

formally identical to the mirror equation, but $d_i$ is measured on the transmitted side.

**Interpretation:** parallel incident rays converge to (converging lens) or diverge from (diverging lens, $f \lt 0$) the focal point.

### Spherical Lens Equation

**Result:** for a single spherical refracting surface of radius $R$ separating $n_1$ (object side) from $n_2$ (image side):

$$
    \boxed{\frac{n_1}{d_o}+\frac{n_2}{d_i}=\frac{n_2-n_1}{R}}
$$

with the Cartesian sign convention below. The thin-lens equation follows by applying this twice.

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

A paraxial ray at height $h$ meets the surface with incidence $\approx h/d_o+h/R$ and refraction $\approx h/R-h/d_i$ after small-angle Snell $n_1\theta_1=n_2\theta_2$; rearrangement gives the formula.

</div>

## Lensmaker's Equation

**Result:** for a thin lens of index $n$ in air with surface radii $R_1,R_2$:

$$
    \boxed{\frac1f=(n-1)\left(\frac1{R_1}-\frac1{R_2}\right)}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Apply the spherical-surface formula first with $n_1=1,n_2=n$, then with $n_1=n,n_2=1$, letting the intermediate image serve as the object for the second surface with coincident vertices in the thin limit. Addition eliminates the interior distance and leaves the boxed form.

</div>

**Limit:** a symmetric biconvex lens with $R_1=R$, $R_2=-R$ gives $1/f=2(n-1)/R$.

## Magnification

**Definition:** transverse magnification is the ratio of image height $h_i$ to object height $h_o$:

$$
    \boxed{m=\frac{h_i}{h_o}=-\frac{d_i}{d_o}}
$$

Negative $m$ denotes inversion; $ \lvert m \rvert \gt 1$ denotes enlargement.

**Derivation:** similar triangles between the object, the vertex, and the image give $h_i/h_o=-d_i/d_o$, the sign following the Cartesian convention below.

## Optical Power

**Definition:** the power of a mirror or lens is the reciprocal focal length:

$$
    \boxed{P=\frac1f}
$$

with $[P]=\mathrm m^{-1}$, named the dioptre. Converging elements have $P \gt 0$; diverging elements have $P \lt 0$.

For two thin elements in contact, $P=P_1+P_2$.

## Sign Conventions

This text uses the Cartesian convention throughout: light travels from left to right, distances are measured from the vertex, and:

- $d_o \gt 0$ for a real object on the incident side.
- $d_i \gt 0$ on the transmitted side (lenses) or the reflecting side (mirrors); $d_i \lt 0$ denotes a virtual image on the object side.
- $R \gt 0$ when the center of curvature lies on the transmitted side (lenses) or reflecting side (mirrors).
- $f \gt 0$ for converging lenses and concave mirrors; $f \lt 0$ for diverging lenses and convex mirrors.
- $h_i \gt 0$ is erect; $m \lt 0$ is inverted.

Consistent use of one convention is essential; the older real-is-positive convention differs in signs for mirrors and virtual objects.

## Prism Equation

**Setup:** a ray traverses a prism of apex angle $A$ and index $n$ in air, with incidence $i$, emergence $e$, and internal angles $r_1,r_2$.

**Result:** geometry gives $r_1+r_2=A$, and Snell at each face gives $\sin i=n\sin r_1$, $\sin e=n\sin r_2$. The total deviation is:

$$
    \boxed{\delta=i+e-A}
$$

## Minimum Deviation

**Result:** $\delta(i)$ is minimized for the symmetric path $i=e$, $r_1=r_2=A/2$. The minimum deviation $\delta_m$ then determines $n$:

$$
    \boxed{n=\frac{\sin\left(\frac{A+\delta_m}{2}\right)}{\sin\left(\frac{A}{2}\right)}}
$$

<div class="box" markdown="1">
<div class="box__title">Derivation</div>

Symmetry gives $i=(A+\delta_m)/2$ since $\delta_m=2i-A$, and $r=A/2$; Snell $\sin i=n\sin r$ yields the formula.

</div>

This is the canonical refractometric method: measure $A$ and $\delta_m$, infer $n$.

## Dispersive Power

**Definition:** for indices $n_F,n_D,n_C$ at the corresponding wavelengths:

$$
    \boxed{\omega=\frac{n_F-n_C}{n_D-1}}
$$

The reciprocal $V=1/\omega$ is the Abbe number. Large $\omega$ (small $V$) means strong dispersion; achromatic doublets combine crown and flint glasses to cancel first-order chromatic error.

**Limitation:** the paraxial formulae above use a single $n$; chromatic aberration is precisely their failure for broadband light.
