# Foundations of Probability

## Experiment

An experiment or trial is the mathematical model of any procedure that can be infinitely repeated and has a well-defined set of possible outcomes.

An experiment consists of 3 parts:

- A sample space $ \Omega $
- A space of events $F$
- A probability measure $P$ which assigns to each event $ E \in F $ its probability $ P(E) $

## Outcome

An outcome $ \omega $ is a possible result of an experiment or trial. Each possible outcome of a particular experiment is a unique random element.

Outcomes in a sample space that occur with equal probability are called **equally likely outcomes**.

## Sample Space

A sample space $\Omega$ is the set of all possible outcomes of an experiment. Each outcome is called a sample point of that space.

A set $ \Omega = \{ \omega_1, \omega_2, \dots, \omega_n \} $ is a sample space if it meets the following conditions:

- The outcomes (i.e. $ \omega_1, \omega_2, \dots, \omega_n $) must be **mutually exclusive**. Each outcome must be distinct; only one can happen at a time.
- The outcomes must be **collectively exhaustive**. It must include all possible outcomes of the experiment.
- The outcomes must be **well defined**. Each outcome must be clearly defined so there is no ambiguity.

**Examples:**

- A sample space of tossing a coin once is $ \{ H, T \} $, where $H$ is the outcome where the coin lands heads and $T$ is for tails.
- A sample space of throwing a die is $ \{ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 \} $, where each number represents the number of dots on each side of the die.
- Another possible sample space of throwing a die is $ \{ E, O \} $, where $E$ represents an even side and $O$ represents an odd side.

## Event

An event $E$ is a subset of the sample space to which a probability is assigned (i.e. $ E \subseteq \Omega $). A single outcome may be an element of many different events, and different events in an experiment are usually not equally likely.

An event consisting of only a single outcome is called an **elementary event** or an atomic event (i.e. it is a singleton set). An event that has more than one possible outcome is called a **compound event**. An event that has no outcomes is called an **empty event**. The event where some event $A$ does not occur is $A^c = \Omega \setminus A$.

The **union of events** $A$ and $B$ is $A \cup B$ and it represents the event where either $A$ or $B$ occurs. The **intersection of events** $A$ and $B$ is $A \cap B$ and it represents the event where both $A$ and $B$ occur.

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive** or disjoint if they cannot occur at the same time (i.e. $A \cap B = \emptyset$), in which case,

$$
    P(A \cap B) = 0 \qquad \text{(Mutually exclusive events)}
$$.

Two events $A$ and $B$ are **independent** if the occurrance of one does not change the probability of the other.

$$
    P(A \cap B) = P(A) P(B) \qquad \text{(Independent events)} \\
$$

Or equivalently,

$$
    P(A | B) = P(A)
$$

provided $B > 0$.

Two non-zero probability events cannot be both mutually exclusive and independent.

## Event Space

Event space $F$ is the set of all possible events to which probabilities are assigned.

For a finite sample space, the event space is the power set of the sample space. If a finite sample space $\Omega$ has $n$ outcomes, then the event space has $2^n$ events.

## Probability Axioms

The standard probability axioms are the Kolmogorov axioms, which are the following:

- **First Axiom:** The probability $ P(E) $ of an event $E \in F$ is a non-negative real number.

    $$
        P(E) \geq 0 \quad \text{for all} \; E \in F
    $$

- **Second Axiom:** The probability that one of the elementary events in the entire sample space $ \Omega $ will occur is 1.

    $$
        \boxed{P(\Omega) = 1}
    $$

- **Third Axiom:** Any countable sequence of mutually exclusive events $ E_1, E_2, \dots $ satisfies

    $$
        P(E_1 \cup E_2 \cup \cdots ) = P(E_1) + P(E_2) + \cdots
    $$

## Probability Measure

The probability measure is a mapping $P$ that assigns a probability to each event.

$$
    P: F \rightarrow [0, 1]
$$

The Kolmogorov axioms define a probability measure. Some important properties derived from the axioms are:

- The probability of any event $A$ occurring is a real number between $0$ and $1$.

    $$
        \boxed{0 \leq P(A) \leq 1}
    $$

- The probability of an event $A$ not occurring is

    $$
        \boxed{P(A^c) = 1 - P(A)}
    $$

- The probability of no outcome occurring is zero. 

    $$
        \boxed{P(\emptyset) = 0}
    $$

- If $A \subseteq B$, then $P(A) \leq P(B)$

## Addition Rule of Probability

The probability of either $A$ or $B$ occurring is $P(A \cup B)$, which is given by

$$
    \boxed{P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \cap B)}
$$

where $P(A \cap B)$ is the probability of both $A$ and $B$ occurring together.

If $A$ and $B$ are mutually exclusive, then $P(A \cap B) = 0$. So, the probability of mutually exclusive events $A$ and $B$ occurring is

$$
    P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) \qquad \text{(For mutually exclusive events)}
$$

which is exactly the third Kolmogorov axiom.

The addition rule can be extended to an arbitrary number of events that are not necessarily mutually exclusive. In the case of events $A$, $B$ and $C$, the rule is

$$
    P(A \cup B \cup C) = P(A) + P(B) + P(C) - P(A \cap B) - P(B \cap C) - P(C \cap A) - P(A \cap B \cap C)
$$

## Conditional Probability

Conditional probability is the probability of some event $A$, given the occurrence of some other event $B$. Hence, the probability of $A$, given $B$ is

$$
    \boxed{P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)}}
$$

**Multiplication Rule of Probability**: From the equation of conditional probability, we can derive the multiplication rule of probability, which is

$$
    P(A \cap B) = P(A | B) P(B) = P(B | A) P(A)
$$

## Bayes' Theorem

$$
    \boxed{P(A | B) = \frac{P(B | A) P(A)}{P(B)}}
$$

## Law of Total Probability

The law of total probability is a theorem that states that, if $\{ B_1, B_2, \dots \}$ is a countable set of mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive events, then for any event $A$

$$
    \boxed{P(A) = \sum_i P(A \cap B_i) = \sum_i P(A | B_i) P(B_i)}
$$
