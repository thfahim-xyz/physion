# Vector Spaces

## Vector Space

A vector space (AKA linear space) is a set whose elements (called vectors) can be added together and multiplied by numbers (called scalars), given that the operations of vector addition and scaler multiplication satisfies the vector axioms.

A vector space over a field $\mathbb F$ is a non-empty set $V$ together with a binary operation and a binary function that satisfy the **vector axioms**.

- The elements of $V$ are commonly called **vectors**, and the elements of $\mathbb F$ are called **scalars**.
- The binary operation, called **vector addition**, assigns to any two vectors $\mathbf v, \mathbf w \in V$ a third vector $\mathbf v + \mathbf w \in V$.
- The binary function, called **scalar multiplication**, assigns to any scalar $a \in \mathbb F$ and any vector $\mathbf v \in V$ another vector $a \mathbf v \in V$.

**Vector Axioms:** To have a vector space, the eight following axioms must be satisfied for every $u, v, w \in V$, and $a, b \in F$:

| Axiom                                                                   | Statement                                                                                                                                                       |
|-------------------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| Associativity of vector addition                                        | $\mathbf u + (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) = (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) + \mathbf w$                                                                                     |
| Commutativity of vector addition                                        | $\mathbf u + \mathbf v = \mathbf v + \mathbf u$                                                                                                                 |
| Identity element of vector addition                                     | There exists an element $\mathbf 0 \in V$, called the **zero vector**, such that $\mathbf v + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf v$ for all $\mathbf v \in V$                  |
| Inverse elements of vector addition                                     | For every $\mathbf v \in V$, there exists an element $− \mathbf v \in V$, called the additive inverse of $\mathbf v$, such that $\mathbf v + (− \mathbf v) = 0$ |
| Compatibility of scalar multiplication with field multiplication        | $a (b \mathbf v) = (a b) \mathbf v$                                                                                                                             |
| Identity element of scalar multiplication                               | $1 \mathbf v = \mathbf v$, where $1$ denotes the multiplicative identity in $\mathbb F$                                                                         |
| Distributivity of scalar multiplication with respect to vector addition | $a (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = a \mathbf u + a \mathbf v$                                                                                                         |
| Distributivity of scalar multiplication with respect to field addition  | $(a + b) \mathbf v = a \mathbf v + b \mathbf v$                                                                                                                 |

If $\mathbb F = \mathbb R$, then $V$ is a real vector space. If $\mathbb F = \mathbb C$, then $V$ is a complex vector space.

**Arrows in a plane, ordered pairs of numbers, coordinates, complex numbers, functions, linear equations all form vector spaces and thus, are vectors.**

### Vector Subspace

A vector subspace (AKA linear subspace) is a vector space that is a subset of some larger vector space.

If $V$ is a vector space over a field $\mathbb F$, then a subset $G \subseteq V$ is a linear subspace of $V$ if $G$ is a vector space over $\mathbb F$ for the operations of $V$. That is, $G \subseteq V$ is a linear subspace of $V$ if $G$ is closed under vector addition and scalar multiplication.

### Linear Combination

A linear combination of vectors is a vector formed by multiplying each vector by a scalar and adding the resulting vectors:

For a set of vectors $G = \\{ \mathbf v_1, \ldots, \mathbf v_n \\} \subseteq V$ and a set of scalers $\\{ a_1, \ldots, a_n \\} \subseteq \mathbb F$, their linear combination is another vector $\mathbf v \in V$, given by

$$
    \mathbf v = a_1 \mathbf v_1 + \cdots + a_n \mathbf v_n
$$

### Linear Independence

The elements of a subset $G$ of a vector space $V$ are linearly independent if no element of $G$ can be written as a linear combination of the other elements of $G$.

Equivalently, a set of vectors $G = \\{ \mathbf v_1, \ldots, \mathbf v_n \\}$ is linearly independent if the only solution to

$$
    a_1 \mathbf v_1 + \cdots + a_n \mathbf v _n = \mathbf 0
$$

is $a_1 = \cdots = a_n = 0$ (trivial solution).

Otherwise, the vectors are **linearly dependent**.

### Linear Span

Given a subset $$G = \\{ \mathbf v_1, \ldots, \mathbf v_n \\}$$ of a vector space $V$, the linear span of $G$ is the smallest linear subspace of $V$ that contains $G$ (in the sense that it is the intersection of all linear subspaces that contain $G$).

The span of $G$ is also the set of all linear combinations of elements of $G$. $G$ is called a spanning set (AKA generating set) of $\mathrm{span}(G)$.

$$
    \mathrm{span}(G) = \{ a_1 \mathbf v_1 + \cdots + a_n \mathbf v_n \; \vert \; \mathbf v_1, \ldots, \mathbf v_n \in G, \; a_1, \ldots, a_n \in \mathbb F, \; n \in \mathbb N \}
$$

### Basis and Components

A basis of a vector space is a linearly independent set of vectors whose span is the entire vector space. Every vector in the space has a unique representation as a linear combination of the basis vectors.

A subset $B = \\{ \mathbf e_1, \ldots, \mathbf e_n \\} \subseteq V$ is a basis if $\mathbf e_1, \ldots, \mathbf e_n$ are linearly independent and $\mathrm{span}(B) = V$.

Thus, any vector $\mathbf v \in V$ can be written as a **unique linear combination** of $\mathbf e_1, \ldots, \mathbf e_n$.

$$
    \mathbf v = v_1 \mathbf e_1 + \cdots + v_n \mathbf e_n
$$

where the coefficients $v_1, \ldots, v_n \in \mathbb F$ are called the **components** of $\mathbf v$ relative to the basis $B$

Every vector space has atleast one basis, and many in general.

### Dimension

The dimension of a vector space is the number of vectors in any basis of that space. It measures the number of independent directions or degrees of freedom in the space.

For a vector space $V$ with basis $B$, the dimension of the space is

$$
    \dim V = \lvert B \rvert
$$

The **dimension theorem for vector spaces** states that, all bases of a vector space have the same cardinality (number of elements).

### Direct Sum

The direct sum of two vector spaces $V$ and $W$ is the vector space $V \oplus W$, consisting of ordered pairs $(v, w)$ with $v \in V$ and $w \in W$, equipped with component-wise addition and scalar multiplication. Every element has a unique decomposition

$$
    x = v + w, \qquad v \in V, \; w \in W
$$

Its dimension satisfies

$$
    \dim (V \oplus W) = \dim V + \dim W
$$

### Tensor Product

The tensor product of vector spaces $V$ and $W$ is the vector space $V \otimes W$, generated by formal products $\mathbf v \otimes \mathbf w$, subject to bilinearity:

$$
    (a \mathbf v_1 + b \mathbf v_2) \otimes \mathbf w = a (\mathbf v_1 \otimes \mathbf w) + b (\mathbf v_2 \otimes \mathbf w),
$$

$$
    \mathbf v \otimes (a \mathbf w_1 + b \mathbf w_2) = a (\mathbf v \otimes \mathbf w_1) + b (\mathbf v \otimes \mathbf w_2)
$$

Its dimension satisfies

$$
    \dim (V \otimes W) = \dim V \times \dim W
$$

It provides a natural vector space for representing bilinear and, more generally, multilinear relationships.
