# Limits and Continuity

## Limit

The limit of a function describes what value the function approaches as the input approaches some point. The limit of a function $f(x)$ is $L$ if the values of $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$ (but not necessarily equal to $a$).

Formally, if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < \lvert x - a \rvert < \delta \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L
$$

### One-Sided Limit

One-sided limits are limits where the input is approached from only one side. The two-sided limit of a function exists if and only if both left-hand and right-hand limits exist and are equal.

$$
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = L
$$

**Left-Hand Limit:** Left-hand limit is the limit where the input is approached from the "left" side.

Formally, if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that $a - \delta < x < a \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to a^{-}} f(x) = L
$$

**Right-Hand Limit:** Left-hand limit is the limit where the input is approached from the "right" side.

Formally, if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that $a < x < a + \delta \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to a^{+}} f(x) = L
$$

### Limits at Infinity

Limits at infinity describe the behaviour of a function when the input becomes arbitrarily large (positive or negative).

Formally, if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a number $N$ such that $x > N \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to \infty} f(x) = L
$$

Similarly, if for every $\varepsilon > 0$, there exists a number $N$ such that $x < -N \implies \lvert f(x) - L \rvert < \varepsilon$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to -\infty} f(x) = L
$$

### Infinite Limits

Infinite limits are the limits where the function itself becomes arbitrarily large or arbitrarily small.

Formally, if for every $M > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < \lvert x - a \rvert < \delta \implies f(x) > M$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty
$$

Likewise, if for every $M > 0$, there exists a $\delta > 0$ such that $0 < \lvert x - a \rvert < \delta \implies f(x) < -M$, then

$$
    \lim_{x \to a} f(x) = -\infty
$$

### Properties of Limits

- Linearity:

    $$
        \lim [a f(x) + b g(x)] = a \lim f(x) + b \lim g(x)
    $$

- Multiplicativity:

    $$
        \lim [f(x) g(x)] = \lim f(x) \cdot \lim g(x)
    $$

- Power Rule:

    $$
        \lim [f(x)]^n = [\lim f(x)]^n
    $$

- Constant Rule:

    $$
        \lim c = c
    $$

- Identity Rule:

    $$
        \lim_{x \to a} x = a
    $$

- Composition Rule:

    $$
        \lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f \left( \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \right)
    $$

    if $f$ is continuous at $\lim_{x \to a} g(x)$

## Limits of Common Functions

- $$\lim_{x \to 0} (1 + x)^{1/x} = \lim_{x \to \infty} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{x} \right)^x = e$$

- $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{1}{x} = 0$$

- $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\ln (1 + x)}{x} = 1$$

- $$\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\ln x}{x} = 0$$

- $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin x}{x} = 1$$

- $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x} = 0$$

- $$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1 - \cos x}{x^2} = \frac{1}{2}$$

## Continuity

A function $f(x)$ is continuous at a point $c$ if

$$
    \lim_{x \to c} f(x) = f(c)
$$

### Types of Discontinuities

- Removable Discontinuity
- Jump Discontinuity
- Infinite Discontinuity

## Intermediate Value Theorem

The intermediate value theorem states that if $f$ is a continuous function whose domain contains the interval $[a, b]$ and $s$ is a number such that $f(a) < s < f(b)$, then there exists some $x$ between $a$ and $b$ such that $f(x) = s$.
