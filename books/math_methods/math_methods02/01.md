# Vector Algebra and Geometry

## Vectors

A vector is an object that has both magnitude and direction. It is a generalization of a single number. Generally, vectors are elements of a vector space.

In introductory physics, vectors can be represented geometrically using Euclidean vectors, which are arrows in space whose length and direction represent the magnitude and direction of a vector. Euclidean vectors and be added together using the **triangle law of vector addition**. They are also translation invariant (i.e. they can be moved freely without changing their direction)

### Components and Basis Vectors

A Vector can be represented using a linear combination of components and basis vectors. The components and basis vectors used to represent a vector can change under coordinate transformations, but vectors themselves are abstract geometric objects which do not change under such transformations.

### Kronecker Delta

$$
    \boxed{
        \delta_{ij} = 
        \begin{cases} 
            0 & \text{if } i \neq j \\
            1 & \text{if } i = j 
        \end{cases}
    }
$$

Kronecker delta represents the identity matrix. It also represents the metric in Cartesian coordinates. In an orthonormal basis, Kronecker delta is defined by

$$
    \hat{\mathbf e}_i \cdot \hat{\mathbf e}_j = \delta_{ij}
$$

- $$ \delta_{ij} A_i = A_j $$

- $$ \delta_{ij} = \delta_{ji} $$

- $$ \delta_{ii} = n \qquad \text{(For n-dimensional space)} $$

- $$ \delta_{ij} \delta_{jk} = \delta_{ik} $$

### Levi-Civita Symbol

$$
    \boxed{
        \varepsilon_{ijk} = 
        \begin{cases} 
            +1 & \text{for even permutation of } (1, 2, 3) \\
            -1 & \text{for odd permutation of } (1, 2, 3) \\
            0 & \text{for repeated indices} 
        \end{cases}
    }
$$

In an orthonormal basis, Levi-Civita symbol is defined by

$$
    (\hat{\mathbf e}_i \times \hat{\mathbf e}_j) = \varepsilon_{ijk} \hat{\mathbf e}_k 
    \implies \varepsilon_{ijk} = (\hat{\mathbf e}_i \times \hat{\mathbf e}_j) \cdot \hat{\mathbf e}_k
$$

- $$ \boxed{\varepsilon_{ijk} = \varepsilon_{jik}} \\ \implies \varepsilon_{ijk} = \varepsilon_{jik} \\ \implies \varepsilon_{iik} = 0 $$

- The product of two Levi-Civita symbols is

    $$
        \varepsilon_{ijk} \varepsilon_{lmn} = 
        \left|
            \begin{matrix}
                \delta_{il} & \delta_{im} & \delta_{in} \\
                \delta_{jl} & \delta_{jm} & \delta_{jn} \\
                \delta_{kl} & \delta_{km} & \delta_{kn}
            \end{matrix}
        \right|
    $$
    
    $$
        \implies \boxed{\varepsilon_{ijk} \varepsilon_{imn} = \delta_{jm} \delta_{kn} - \delta_{jn} \delta_{km}}
    $$

    $$
        \implies \varepsilon_{ijk} \varepsilon_{mjk} = 2 \delta_{im}
    $$

- $$ \boxed{\varepsilon_{ijk} \delta_{jk} = 0} $$

## Vector Operations

### Vector Addition

**Properties:**

- Commutativity: $ \mathbf u + \mathbf v = \mathbf v + \mathbf u $
- Associativity: $ (\mathbf u + \mathbf v) + \mathbf w = \mathbf u + (\mathbf v + \mathbf w) $
- Identity element (zero vector): $ \mathbf v + \mathbf 0 = \mathbf v $
- Inverse elements (additive inverse): $ \mathbf v + (-\mathbf v) = \mathbf 0$

### Scaler Multiplication

**Properties:**

- Commutativity: $ a \mathbf v = \mathbf v a $
- Associativity: $ a(b \mathbf v) = (ab) \mathbf v $
- Identity element (unit scaler): $ 1 \mathbf v = \mathbf v $
- Distributivity over vector addition: $ a(\mathbf u + \mathbf v) = a \mathbf u + a \mathbf v $
- Distributivity over scaler addition: $ (a + b) \mathbf v = a \mathbf v + b \mathbf v $
- $ 0 \mathbf v = \mathbf 0 $
- $ -1 \mathbf v = - \mathbf v $

### Dot Product

$$
    \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \Vert \mathbf u \Vert \Vert \mathbf v \Vert \cos \theta
$$

**Properties:**

- Bilinearity: $ \mathbf u \cdot (a \mathbf v + b \mathbf w) = a \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v + b \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf w $
- Commutativity: $ \mathbf u \cdot \mathbf v = \mathbf v \cdot \mathbf u $

Dot product can be used to define the orthogonality, magnitude and projection of vectors.

## Common Coordinate Systems

### Cartesian Coordinate System

In Cartesian coordinates, points are labelled by $ (x, y, z) $

The basis vectors are:

$$
    \hat{\mathbf x} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial x}, \quad
    \hat{\mathbf y} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial y}, \quad
    \hat{\mathbf z} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial z}
$$

where $ \mathbf r = \mathbf r (x, y, z) $ is the position vector in Cartesian coordinates.

The differential line element is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf r = \mathrm d x \, \hat{\mathbf x} + \mathrm d y \, \hat{\mathbf y} + \mathrm d z \, \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

The differential normal area is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf S = \mathrm d y \, \mathrm d z \, \hat{\mathbf x} + \mathrm d z \, \mathrm d x \, \hat{\mathbf y} + \mathrm d x \, \mathrm d y \, \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

The differential volume element is

$$
    \mathrm d V = \mathrm d x \, \mathrm d y \, \mathrm d z
$$

### Cylindrical Coordinate System

In cylindrical coordinates, points are labelled by $ (\rho, \theta, z) $, where

- $ \rho $ is the radial distance or radius $ (r \geq 0) $
- $ \theta $ is the azimuth $ (0 \leq \theta \leq 2 \pi) $
- $ z $ is the axial coordinate $ (-\infty \leq z \leq \infty) $

The convertion from Cartesian to cylindrical coordinates is done using the following equations:

$$
    \rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}
$$

$$
    \varphi = \arctan \left( \frac{y}{x} \right)
$$

$$
    z = z
$$

The (unit) basis vectors are:

$$
    \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial \rho}, \quad
    \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial \theta}, \quad
    \hat{\mathbf z} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial z}
$$

where $ \mathbf r = \mathbf r (\rho, \theta, z) $ is the position vector in cylindrical coordinates.

The line element is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf r = \mathrm d \rho \, \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + r \, \mathrm d \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \mathrm d z \, \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

The differential normal area is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf S = \rho \, \mathrm d \varphi \, \mathrm d z \, \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + \mathrm d \rho \, \mathrm d z \, \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} + \rho \, \mathrm d \rho \, \mathrm d \varphi \, \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

The volume element is

$$
    \mathrm d V = \rho \, \mathrm d \rho \, \mathrm d \theta \, \mathrm d z
$$

### Spherical Coordinate System

In spherical coordinates, points are labelled by $ (r, \theta, \varphi) $, where

- $ r $ is the radial distance or radius $ (r \geq 0) $
- $ \theta $ is the polar angle $ (0 \leq \theta \leq \pi) $
- $ \varphi $ is the azimuthal angle $ (0 \leq z < 2 \pi) $

The convertion from Cartesian to spherical coordinates is done using the following equations:

$$
    r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}
$$

$$
    \theta = \arccos \left( \frac{z}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}} \right)
$$

$$
    \varphi = \arctan \left( \frac{y}{x} \right)
$$

The (unit) basis vectors are:

$$
    \hat{\mathbf r} = \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial r}, \quad
    \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} = \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial \theta}, \quad
    \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} = \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial \mathbf r}{\partial \varphi}
$$

where $ \mathbf r = \mathbf r (r, \theta, \varphi) $ is the position vector in spherical coordinates.

The line element is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf r = \mathrm d r \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \, \mathrm d \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + r \sin \theta \, \mathrm d \varphi \, \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}
$$

The differential normal area is

$$
    \mathrm d \mathbf S = r^2 \sin \theta \, \mathrm d \theta \, \mathrm d \varphi \, \hat{\mathbf r} + r \sin \theta \, \mathrm d r \, \mathrm d \varphi \, \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + r \, \mathrm d r \, \mathrm d \theta \, \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}
$$

The volume element is

$$
    \mathrm d V = r^2 \sin \theta \, \mathrm d r \, \mathrm d \theta \, \mathrm d \varphi
$$
