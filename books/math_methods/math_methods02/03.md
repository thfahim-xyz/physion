# Multivariable Differentiation

## Partial Derivative

The partial derivative $\frac{\partial}{\partial x} \equiv \partial_x$ of a function $f(x, y, z)$ with respect to $x$ describes the rate of change of $f$ as $x$ changes, holding other variables constant.

$$
    \boxed{\frac{\partial f}{\partial x} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{f(x + \Delta x, y, z) - f(x, y, z)}{\Delta x}}
$$

## Total Derivative

The total derivative $\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm d t}$ of a function $f(x(t), y(t), z(t))$ with respect to $t$ describes the total rate of change of $f$ as all of its variables change. Total derivative is defined using the **multivariable chain rule**:

$$
    \frac{\mathrm d f}{\mathrm d t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \frac{\mathrm d x}{\mathrm d t}
                                    + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \frac{\mathrm d y}{\mathrm d t}
                                    + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \frac{\mathrm d z}{\mathrm d t}
$$

### Total Differential

Total Differential is often viewed as the most fundamental form of the total derivative. It describes the infinitesimal change in a function $f(x, y, z)$ as a result of change in each variable.

$$
    \mathrm d f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \mathrm d x 
                + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \mathrm d y
                + \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} \mathrm d z
$$

In general, for any function $f(x_1, \dots, x_n)$, the total differential is

$$
    \boxed{\mathrm d f = \sum_i \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i} \mathrm d x_i}
$$

Total differential is related to the gradient by

$$
    \boxed{\mathrm d f = \nabla f \cdot \mathrm d \mathbf r}
$$

where $\mathrm d \mathbf r$ is the total differential of the position vector.

## Vector Derivative Operator

In Cartesian coordinates,

$$
    \boxed{\nabla = \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \hat{\mathbf x} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \hat{\mathbf y} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \hat{\mathbf z}}
$$

In cylindrical coordinates,

$$
    \nabla = \frac{\partial}{\partial \rho} \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial}{\partial \varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} + \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

In spherical coordinates,

$$
    \nabla = \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \hat{\mathbf r} + \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial \theta} \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial}{\partial \varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}
$$

## Gradient

The gradient of a scalar field gives the direction and rate of the steepest increase of that field.

For a scaler field $\phi = \phi(x, y, z)$ in Cartesian coordinates, the gradient is

$$
    \boxed{\nabla \phi = \dfrac{\partial \phi}{\partial x} \hat{\mathbf x} + \dfrac{\partial \phi}{\partial y} \hat{\mathbf y} + \dfrac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} \hat{\mathbf z}}
$$

For a scaler field $\phi = \phi(\rho, \varphi, z)$ in cylindrical coordinates, the gradient is

$$
    \nabla = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \rho} \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} + \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial z} \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

For a scaler field $\phi = \phi(r, \theta, \varphi)$ in spherical coordinates, the gradient is

$$
    \nabla = \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial r} \hat{\mathbf r} + \frac{1}{r} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \theta} \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial \phi}{\partial \varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}
$$

**Properties:**

- $$\nabla (\psi + \phi) = \nabla \psi + \nabla \phi$$
- $$\nabla (\psi \phi) = (\nabla \psi) \phi + \psi \nabla \phi$$

## Directional Derivative

The directional derivative $D_{\hat{\mathbf v}}$ of the function $\phi$ describes the gradient or rate of change of $\phi$ in the direction of $\hat{\mathbf v}$.

$$
    D_{\hat{\mathbf v}} = \hat{\mathbf v} \cdot \nabla \phi
$$

## Divergence

The divergence of a vector field measures how much the field behaves like a source or sink at a point (net flow out per unit volume).

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_x \hat{\mathbf x} + A_y \hat{\mathbf y} + A_z \hat{\mathbf z}$ in Cartesian coordinates, the divergence is

$$
    \boxed{\nabla \cdot \mathbf A = \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}}
$$

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_{\rho} \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + A_{\varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} + A_z \hat{\mathbf z}$ in cylindrical coordinates, the divergence is

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf A = \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial (\rho A_{\rho})}{\partial \rho} + \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial A_{\varphi}}{\partial \varphi} + \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial z}
$$

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_r \hat{\mathbf r} + A_{\theta} \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + A_{\varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}$ in spherical coordinates, the divergence is

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf A = \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial (r^2 A_r)}{\partial r} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial (A_{\theta} \sin \theta)}{\partial \theta} + \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \frac{\partial A_{\varphi}}{\partial \varphi}
$$

**Properties:**

- $$ \nabla \cdot (\mathbf A + \mathbf B) = \nabla \cdot \mathbf A + \nabla \cdot \mathbf B $$

- $$ \nabla \cdot (\phi \mathbf A) = (\nabla \phi) \cdot \mathbf A + \phi \, \nabla \cdot \mathbf A $$

- $$ \boxed{\nabla \cdot (\mathbf A \times \mathbf B) = (\nabla \times \mathbf A) \cdot \mathbf B - \mathbf A \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf B)} $$

- $$ \boxed{\nabla \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf A) = 0} $$

## Curl

The curl of a vector field measures the field’s local tendency to rotate (circulation per unit area around a point).

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_x \hat{\mathbf x} + A_y \hat{\mathbf y} + A_z \hat{\mathbf z}$ in Cartesian coordinates, the curl is

$$
    \boxed{
    \nabla \times \mathbf A 
    =
    \left|
    \begin{matrix}
        \hat{\mathbf x} & \hat{\mathbf y} & \hat{\mathbf z} \\
        \frac{\partial}{\partial x} & \frac{\partial}{\partial y} & \frac{\partial}{\partial z} \\
        A_x & A_y & A_z
    \end{matrix}
    \right|
    = \left( \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial y} - \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial z} \right) \hat{\mathbf x} 
    + \left( \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial x} \right) \hat{\mathbf y} 
    + \left( \frac{\partial A_y}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial A_x}{\partial y} \right) \hat{\mathbf z}
    }
$$

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_{\rho} \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} + A_{\varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} + A_z \hat{\mathbf z}$ in cylindrical coordinates, the curl is

$$
    \nabla \times \mathbf A 
    = \left( \frac{1}{\rho} \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial \varphi} - \frac{\partial A_{\varphi}}{\partial z} \right) \hat{\boldsymbol \rho} 
    + \left( \frac{\partial A_{\rho}}{\partial z} - \frac{\partial A_z}{\partial \rho} \right) \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi} 
    + \frac{1}{\rho} \left( \frac{\partial (\rho A_{\varphi})}{\partial \rho} - \frac{\partial A_{\rho}}{\partial \varphi} \right) \hat{\mathbf z}
$$

For a vector field $\mathbf A = A_r \hat{\mathbf r} + A_{\theta} \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} + A_{\varphi} \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}$ in spherical coordinates, the curl is

$$
    \nabla \times \mathbf A 
    = \frac{1}{r \sin \theta} \left( \frac{\partial (A_{\varphi} \sin \theta)}{\partial \theta} - \frac{\partial A_{\theta}}{\partial \varphi} \right) \hat{\mathbf r} 
    + \frac{1}{r} \left( \frac{1}{\sin \theta} \frac{\partial A_r}{\partial \varphi} - \frac{\partial (r A_{\varphi})}{\partial r} \right) \hat{\boldsymbol \theta} 
    + \frac{1}{r} \left( \frac{\partial (r A_{\theta})}{\partial r} - \frac{\partial A_r}{\partial \theta} \right) \hat{\boldsymbol \varphi}
$$

**Properties:**

- $$ \nabla \times (\mathbf A + \mathbf B) = \nabla \times \mathbf A + \nabla \times \mathbf B $$

- $$ \nabla \times (\phi \mathbf A) = (\nabla \phi) \times \mathbf A + \phi \, \nabla \times \mathbf A $$

- $$ \boxed{\nabla \times (\nabla \phi) = \mathbf 0} $$

- $$ \boxed{\nabla \times (\mathbf A \times \mathbf B) = (\nabla \cdot \mathbf B) \mathbf A - (\nabla \cdot \mathbf A) \mathbf B + (\mathbf B \cdot \nabla) \mathbf A - (\mathbf A \cdot \nabla) \mathbf B} $$

- $$ \boxed{\nabla \times (\nabla \times \mathbf A) = \nabla (\nabla \cdot \mathbf A) - \nabla^2 \mathbf A} $$

## Jacobian

The Jacobian $J_{\mathbf F} \equiv \nabla \mathbf F \equiv \frac{\partial (f_1, \dots, f_m)}{\partial (x_1, \dots, x_n)}$ of a function $\mathbf F(\mathbf x) = (f_1 (\mathbf x), \dots, f_m (\mathbf x))$, where $\mathbf x = (x_1, \dots, x_n)$, is a matrix whose elements are $J_{ij} = \frac{\partial f_i}{\partial x_j}$. Explicitly,

$$
    J_{\mathbf F} = 
    \left[
    \begin{matrix}
        \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_1} & \dots & \dfrac{\partial f_1}{\partial x_n} \\
        \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
        \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_1} & \dots & \dfrac{\partial f_m}{\partial x_n}
    \end{matrix}
    \right]
$$

The Jacobian matrix encodes divergence and curl of the function inside it. The main diagonal of the Jacobian represents the divergence and the antisymmetric part represents the curl of the function. Therefore, the divergence and curl of $\mathbf F$ can be found from the Jacobian matrix by

$$
    \nabla \cdot \mathbf F = \operatorname{tr}(A)
$$

$$
    \nabla \times \mathbf F = \frac{1}{2} \left( J - J^{\mathsf T} \right)
$$

The Jacobian matrix can be decomposed as

$$
    J = \frac{1}{3} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf F) I 
        + \frac{1}{2} \left( J - J^{\mathsf T} \right) 
        + \left[ \frac{1}{2} \left( J + J^{\mathsf T} \right) - \frac{1}{3} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf F) I \right]
$$

where

- $\dfrac{1}{3} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf F) I$ describes expansion or compression.

- $\dfrac{1}{2} \left( J - J^{\mathsf T} \right)$ describes rotation (curl).

- $\left[ \dfrac{1}{2} \left( J + J^{\mathsf T} \right) - \dfrac{1}{3} (\nabla \cdot \mathbf F) I \right]$ describes shearing or deformation.

For a function $\mathbf F(x, y, z) = (f_x (x, y, z), f_y (x, y, z), f_z (x, y, z))$ in 3D space, the Jacobian matrix is

$$
    J_{\mathbf F} = 
    \left[
    \begin{matrix}
        \dfrac{\partial f_x}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_x}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial f_x}{\partial z} \\
        \dfrac{\partial f_y}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_y}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial f_y}{\partial z} \\
        \dfrac{\partial f_z}{\partial x} & \dfrac{\partial f_z}{\partial y} & \dfrac{\partial f_z}{\partial z}
    \end{matrix}
    \right]
$$
